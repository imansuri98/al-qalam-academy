### **Module 1: The Building Blocks (أَجْزَاءُ الجُمْلَةِ) \- Ism, Fil, Harf** {#module-1:-the-building-blocks-(أَجْزَاءُ-الجُمْلَةِ)---ism,-fil,-harf}

#### **Lesson 1.1:** The Useful Sentence which gives complete meaning  (الجُمْلَةُ المُفِيدَةُ {#lesson-1.1:-the-useful-sentence-which-gives-complete-meaning-(الجُمْلَةُ-المُفِيدَةُ}

Ever wondered how words talk to each other? Just like LEGO bricks, words need to be snapped together in the right way to build something amazing. If you just hold one brick, you can’t build a house. You need a plan\!

Have you ever said a word that left everyone waiting for more? Like if you walked into a room and just said "The book..." and walked out? Everyone would be confused\! That is because a single word like “book” or a broken phrase like “book of zaid” doesn't tell a story.

The Building Blocks

In Arabic, every single word you speak falls into one of three buckets:

1. **اَلِاسْمُ (Noun):** A name of a person, place, thing, or idea.  
2. **اَلْفِعْلُ (Verb):** An action or something happening at a specific time.  
3. **اَلْحَرْفُ (Particle):** A tiny word like "in" or "from."

From Bricks to Walls

If you take a **حَرْفٌ** (Particle) like **فِي** (in), it has a meaning in your head, but it doesn't give us any "Useful Benefit" (**فَائِدَةٌ**). If you say "In the house," (**فِي الْبَيْتِ**) it is better, but it is still an incomplete building (**مُرَكَّبٌ نَاقِصٌ**). We are still waiting to hear *who* is in the house or *what* is happening there\!

A **الجُمْلَةُ الْمُفِيدَةُ** (The Useful Sentence) is when you combine these blocks so the listener is satisfied and doesn't need to ask "And then what?"

* Incomplete: "The fast horse..." (What about it?)  
* Complete: "The horse is fast." (Now I understand\!)  
  Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)  
1. **الجُمْلَةُ الْمُفِيدَةُ**: The combination of words that provides a complete, meaningful benefit to the listener.  
   

#### **Lesson 1.2 :** Built vs. Flexible (المَبْنِيُّ وَالمُعْرَبُ) {#lesson-1.2-:-built-vs.-flexible-(المَبْنِيُّ-وَالمُعْرَبُ)}

Have you ever noticed that some things in life never change no matter where they are, while others adapt to their surroundings? Think about a heavy stone in a garden versus a flexible plant. No matter how much it rains or how the wind blows, the stone stays the same. But the plant might lean or grow differently depending on the sun and water.

Did you know that words in Arabic are just like that? Some are like "stones" that never change their endings, and some are like "plants" that change their vowels based on their position in a sentence.

Look at these examples:

1. **جَاءَ هَؤُلَاءِ الرِّجَالُ**  
2. **رَأَيْتُ هَؤُلَاءِ الرِّجَالُ**  
3. **مَرَرْتُ بِهَؤُلَاءِ الرِّجَالِ**

Now look at these:

1. **جَاءَ زَيْدٌ**  
2. **رَأَيْتُ زَيْدًا**  
3. **مَرَرْتُ بِزَيْدٍ**

In Arabic, most nouns are flexible. Their last letter changes its vowel (**حَرَكَةٌ**) depending on whether the word is the one doing the action, receiving the action, or coming after a particle. This flexibility is called **اَلإِعْرَابُ**.

On the other hand, some words are stubborn\! No matter where you put them in a sentence, the last letter always sounds exactly the same. This "fixed" state is called **اَلْبِنَاءُ**. Think of it like a building (**بِنَاءٌ**) that is cemented into the ground; it cannot move or change.

---

Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)

1. **اَلْمُعْرَبُ**: A word whose last letter changes its vowel (**حَرَكَةٌ**) due to the change of its position or the words coming before it. Most **أَسْمَاءٌ** (nouns) are in this category.  
2. **اَلْمَبْنِيُّ**: A word whose last letter remains in one state and never changes, regardless of its position in the sentence. This includes all **حُرُوفٌ** (particles), certain types of nouns (like pronouns), and some verbs.  
3. The change in **اَلْمُعْرَبُ** is usually between **ضَمَّةٌ**, **فَتْحَةٌ**, and **كَسْرَةٌ**.

   #### Lesson 1.3: Introduction to the Four Signs (عَلَامَاتُ الإِعْرَابِ) {#lesson-1.3:-introduction-to-the-four-signs-(عَلَامَاتُ-الإِعْرَابِ)}

How do you know who is doing what in a story? In English, we usually know by the order of the words. If I say "The boy bit the apple," you know the boy did the biting because he came first.

But in Arabic, words are like people wearing specific **badges**. These badges tell us their job, no matter where they stand in the sentence\! These badges are the small vowels at the very end of the word.

Look at these examples:

1. **قَالَ الْمُعَلِّمُ**: (The teacher said). The **ضَمَّةٌ** tells us the teacher is the speaker.  
2. **رَأَيْتُ الْمُعَلِّمَ**: (I saw the teacher). The **فَتْحَةٌ** tells us the teacher was the one seen.  
3. **سَلَّمْتُ عَلَى الْمُعَلِّمِ**: (I gave greetings to the teacher). The **كَسْرَةٌ** appears because of the word "to" (**عَلَى**).

In English, we see this with words like "Who" (**رَفْعٌ**) and "Whom" (**نَصْبٌ/جَرٌّ**). In Arabic, we use these four "States" to keep everything organized.

---

Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)

1. **اَلرَّفْعُ (Raf’):** The state of the Subject (the one doing the action). Its primary sign is the **ضَمَّةٌ** (—ُ).  
2. **اَلنَّصْبُ (Nasb):** The state of the Object (the one receiving the action). Its primary sign is the **فَتْحَةٌ** (—َ).  
3. **اَلْجَرُّ (Jarr):** The state of a word following a particle (like "in" or "with"). Its primary sign is the **كَسْرَةٌ** (—ِ). **This is only for nouns.**  
4. **اَلْجَزْمُ (Jazm):** The state of a verb being "stopped" or commanded. Its primary sign is the **سُكُونٌ** (—ْ). **This is only for verbs.**

Mastering Raf’, Nasb, Jarr, and Jazm in two specific ways constitutes 95% of Arabic grammar (Nahw):

1\. The "Who": Which words take these states?  
Raf’ and Nasb: Apply to both Nouns and Present Tense Verbs.

Jarr: Applies exclusively to Nouns.

Jazm: Applies exclusively to Present Tense Verbs.

2\. The "How": How are these states expressed?  
Primary Signs: Expressed through short vowels (Damma, Fatha, Kasra) and the Sukun.

Secondary Signs: Expressed through letters (Alif, Waw, Ya, Noon) or the removal of letters.

#### **Lesson 1.4:** Three Root Letter System — *Unique part of Arabic.* {#lesson-1.4:-three-root-letter-system-—-unique-part-of-arabic.}

Have you ever seen a tree where all the branches, leaves, and fruits come from the same hidden root? In Arabic, words grow exactly like that\! Most words aren't just random sounds; they are part of a big family that shares the same "DNA."

This "DNA" usually consists of three core letters called the **الحروف الاصلية** (Root). Imagine having the letters **ك \- ت \- ب**. By just changing the vowels or adding a few 	 extra letters around them, you can create a library of meanings, all related to the idea of "writing."

Look at this family:

* **كَتَبَ** (He wrote)  
* **كِتَابٌ** (A book)  
* **مَكْتَبٌ** (A desk/office)  
* **كَاتِبٌ** (A writer)

In English, the words "book," "write," and "desk" look nothing alike. You have to memorize three different words. But in Arabic, if you know the three root letters, you can often guess what a new word means even if you’ve never seen it before\!

Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)

1.  **الحروف الاصلية**: Most Arabic words are derived from a "Triple Root" consisting of three original letters.  
2. **اَلْفِعْلُ الْمَاضِي**: The root is usually found in the simplest form of the verb (the 3rd person masculine past tense), like **فَعَلَ**.  
3. **الْمِيزَانُ الصَّرْفِيُّ**: Scholars use the pattern **ف \- ع \- ل** (Fa-’A-La) as a scale to measure words. The first letter is the **فَاءُ الْفِعْلِ**, the second is **عَيْنُ الْفِعْلِ**, and the third is **لَامُ الْفِعْلِ,** so in كتب kaaf is fa letter, taa is ein letter, and baa is laam letter.  
4. By adding extra letters (like **م**, **ا**, or **ت**) to the root, we change the meaning while keeping the core "flavor" of the root.

   ### **Module 2: Properties of the Noun (خَصَائِصُ الاِسْمِ)** {#module-2:-properties-of-the-noun-(خَصَائِصُ-الاِسْمِ)}

#### **Lesson 2.1:** Gender (الجِنْسُ): Masculine (المُذَكَّرُ) vs. Feminine (المُؤَنَّثُ). {#lesson-2.1:-gender-(الجِنْسُ):-masculine-(المُذَكَّرُ)-vs.-feminine-(المُؤَنَّثُ).}

In English, if you see a chair or a pen, you call it "it." It is neutral. But in the world of Arabic, every single noun has a personality\! Everything—from the sun in the sky to the shoes on your feet—is treated as either a "he" (**مُذَكَّرٌ**) or a "she" (**مُؤَنَّثٌ**).

Imagine the Arabic language as a big family gathering. There is no "neutral" corner to sit in. Every object you point to must be addressed with the correct gender, which affects the words you use around it.

For example:

* **اَلْقَمَرُ** (The moon) is treated as a male.  
* **اَلشَّمْسُ** (The sun) is treated as a female.

Wait, how do we know which is which? Usually, the "default" for words is masculine. To make a word feminine, we often give it a special "bow" or "hat" at the end, most commonly the **تَاء مَرْبُوطَة** (ة).

In English, we sometimes do this with people (Actor vs. Actress, Waiter vs. Waitress), but in Arabic, we do this for almost everything\!

Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)

1. **اَلْمُذَكَّرُ (Masculine):** Any noun that does not have the signs of being feminine. This is the "starting point" for nouns.  
2. **اَلْمُؤَنَّثُ (Feminine):** This is divided into two main types:  
   * **True Feminine:** Humans or animals that are biologically female (e.g., **أُمٌّ** \- Mother).	  
   * **Grammatical Feminine:** Objects that Arabic speakers decided to treat as female eg..  
3. **Signs of Femininity (عَلَامَاتُ التَّأْنِيثِ):** The most famous sign is the **تَاء مَرْبُوطَة** (**ة**) added to the end of a word.  
   * Example: **مُعَلِّمٌ** (Male teacher) → **مُعَلِّمَةٌ** (Female teacher)  
   * Alif mamduda.  
4. **Body Parts in Pairs:** Generally, any part of the body that you have two of (eyes, ears, hands, feet) is treated as feminine.

#### **Lesson 2.2:** Number (العَدَدُ): Singular (المُفْرَدُ), Dual (المُثَنَّى), and Plural (الجَمْعُ) {#lesson-2.2:-number-(العَدَدُ):-singular-(المُفْرَدُ),-dual-(المُثَنَّى),-and-plural-(الجَمْعُ)}

In English, we usually have one or many. If you have one cat, it is "singular," and if you have two, three, or a hundred, they are all "plural."

But Arabic is more precise\! It has a special "VIP room" just for the number **two**. In Arabic, we distinguish between one person, a pair of people, and a big group. This allows you to know exactly how many things someone is talking about just by the ending of a single word.

Imagine you are at a fruit stand:

* **تُفَّاحَةٌ**: (One apple) — **Singular**.  
* **تُفَّاحَتَانِ**: (Two apples) — **Dual**.  
* **تُفَّاحَاتٌ**: (Many apples) — **Plural**.

Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)

1. **اَلْمُفْرَدُ (Singular):** A noun that refers to only one person or thing.  
2. **اَلْمُثَنَّى (Dual):** A noun that refers to exactly two. It is formed by adding **ـَانِ** (in the state of **رَفْعٌ**) or **ـَيْنِ** (in the states of **نَصْبٌ** or **جَرٌّ**) to the end of the singular word.  
3. **اَلْجَمْعُ (Plural):** A noun that refers to three or more. It has three main types:  
   * **جَمْعُ الْمُذَكَّرِ السَّالِمُ (Sound Masculine Plural):** Formed by adding **ـُونَ(when in the state of رَفْعٌ)** or **ـِينَ (when in the states of نَصْبٌ or جَرٌّ)** (e.g., **مُسْلِمُونَ**) and another eg.  
   * **جَمْعُ الْمُؤَنَّثِ السَّالِمُ (Sound Feminine Plural):** Formed by adding **ـَاتٌ** (e.g., **مُسْلِمَاتٌ**).  
   * **جَمْعُ التَّكْسِيرِ (Broken Plural):** The "rebel" plural. It doesn't follow a fixed ending; instead, it breaks the singular shape (e.g., **كِتَابٌ** becomes **كُتُبٌ**) somemore example.

#### **Lesson 2.3:** Definiteness (التَّعْيِينُ): Indefinite (النَّكِرَةُ) vs. Definite (المَعْرِفَةُ). {#lesson-2.3:-definiteness-(التَّعْيِينُ):-indefinite-(النَّكِرَةُ)-vs.-definite-(المَعْرِفَةُ).}

Imagine you are a tourist in a new city.

* If you tell a taxi driver, "Take me to **a** masjid" (**مَسْجِدٌ**), he can take you to any of the 100 masjids in the city. That is **نَكِرَةٌ** (Indefinite).  
* If you say, "Take me to **the** masjid" (**اَلْمَسْجِدُ**), he knows exactly which one you mean—perhaps the big one in the center of town. That is **مَعْرِفَةٌ** (Definite).

In Arabic, the difference is like a light switch. A noun without the "light" of **الـ** is general and carries **تَنْوِينٌ** (un/an/in). When you flip the switch by adding **الـ**, the noun becomes bright and specific, and the **تَنْوِينٌ** disappears because it cannot stay in the "light."

---

Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)

1. **اَلنَّكِرَةُ (Indefinite):** A noun that is general. It is marked by **تَنْوِينٌ** (double vowels) at the end.  
   * Example: **طَبِيبٌ** (A doctor—could be any doctor).  
2. **اَلْمَعْرِفَةُ (Definite):** A noun that is specific. The most common way to make it specific is adding the prefix **الـ**.  
   * Example: **اَلطَّبِيبُ** (The doctor—a specific one we know).  
3. **The Conflict Rule:** **الـ** and **تَنْوِينٌ** are like oil and water; they never mix. A word either starts with **الـ** or ends with **تَنْوِينٌ**, but never both.  
4. **Automatic Specifics:** Some words don't need **الـ** because they are already famous/specific, like names of people (**زَيْنَبُ**), pronouns (**نَحْنُ** \- We), or pointing words (**تِلْكَ** \- That) some more .

#### **Lesson 2.4:** Flexibility (الصَّرْفُ): Fully Flexible (المُنْصَرِفُ) vs. Diptotes (المَمْنُوعُ مِنَ الصَّرْفِ). {#lesson-2.4:-flexibility-(الصَّرْفُ):-fully-flexible-(المُنْصَرِفُ)-vs.-diptotes-(المَمْنُوعُ-مِنَ-الصَّرْفِ).}

We studied murab and mabni, under murab this category comes: 

In our previous lessons, we saw how most nouns love to change their "outfits." They can wear a **ضَمَّةٌ** for **رَفْعٌ**, a **فَتْحَةٌ** for **نَصْبٌ**, and a **كَسْرَةٌ** for **جَرٌّ**. They also love wearing their **تَنْوِينٌ** (double-vowel) hats\!

But today, we meet a special group of nouns that are a bit more picky. They are like people who refuse to wear certain colors or hats. These are called **المَمْنُوعُ مِنَ الصَّرْفِ** (Diptotes).

Imagine a playground where most kids (**المُنْصَرِفُ**) can go on every slide—the **ضَمَّةٌ** slide, the **فَتْحَةٌ** slide, and the **كَسْرَةٌ** slide. They also love to wear the **تَنْوِينٌ** hat. Then, there is a group of kids (**المَمْنُوعُ مِنَ الصَّرْفِ**) who have two strict rules:

1. They **never** wear the **تَنْوِينٌ** hat.  
2. They **never** go on the **كَسْرَةٌ** slide. Even when they should be in the state of **جَرٌّ**, they wear a **فَتْحَةٌ** instead\!

Rules (قَوَاعِدُ)

1. **اَلْمُنْصَرِفُ (Fully Flexible):** Most Arabic nouns. They accept **تَنْوِينٌ** and can take all three vowels (**ضمة، فتحة، كسرة**).  
2. **اَلْمَمْنُوعُ مِنَ الصَّرْفِ (Diptotes):** Nouns that are "prevented from changing" fully.  
   * They **cannot** take **تَنْوِينٌ**.  
   * In the state of **جَرٌّ**, they take a **فَتْحَةٌ** instead of a **كَسْرَةٌ**.  
3. **Common Diptotes include:**  
   * Many proper names (like **أَحْمَدُ** and **عُمَرُ**).  
   * Names of most cities and countries (like **مَكَّةُ** and **مِصْرُ**).  
   * Certain plural patterns (like **مَسَاجِدُ** and **مَصَابِيحُ**).  
   * Adjectives ending in **ـَان** (like **عَطْشَانُ** \- Thirsty).

In Arabic grammar, a noun becomes a Diptote (اَلْمَمْنُوعُ مِنَ الصَّرْفِ) if it possesses either one strong reason or a combination of two specific reasons.  
1\. The Single Reason (عِلَّةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ)  
Some nouns are so "heavy" they only need one reason to become a diptote.

* The Ultimate Plural (مُنْتَهَى الْجُمُوعِ): Any plural where an Alif is followed by two or three letters.  
  * Patterns: $مَفَاعِلُ$ (e.g., مَسَاجِدُ \- mosques) or $مَفَاعِيْلُ$ (e.g., مَصَابِيْحُ \- lamps).  
* Feminine Alif of Completion (أَلِفُ التَّأْنِيْثِ):  
  * Alif Maqsurah (ـَى): (e.g., حُبْلَى \- pregnant, ذِكْرَى \- memory).  
  * Alif Mamdudah (ـاء): (e.g., صَحْرَاءُ \- desert, حَمْرَاءُ \- red).

---

2\. The Combined Reasons (عِلَّتَانِ)  
Most diptotes require two reasons: a Category (either a Proper Name or an Adjective) plus a Structural/Meaning Trait.  
A. Proper Names (اَلْعَلَمُ) \+ One of the following:

1. Feminine (التَّأْنِيْثُ): Any feminine name, whether ending in Ta Marbuta (فَاطِمَةُ) or not (زَيْنَبُ), and even male names with feminine endings (حَمْزَةُ).  
2. Non-Arabic Origin (الْعُجْمَةُ): Names that are not originally Arabic and have more than three letters or (إِبْرَاهِيْمُ, يُوْسُفُ, لَنْدَنُ) three letters but feminine ().  
3. Compound Words (التَّرْكِيْبُ الْمَزْجِيُّ): Two words fused into one (بَعْلَبَكُّ \- Baalbek).  
4. The "Verb" Pattern (وَزْنُ الْفِعْلِ): Names that look like a verb conjugation (أَحْمَدُ \- like "I praise", يَثْرِبُ).  
5. The "Fu’alu" Pattern (الْعَدْلُ): Names shifted from one scale to another (عُمَرُ, زُحَلُ).  
6. Ending in Alif and Noon (أَلِف وَ نُوْن زَائِدَتَان): Extra suffix added to the root (عُثْمَانُ, رَمَضَانُ).

B. Adjectives (اَلصِّفَةُ) \+ One of the following:

1. Ending in Alif and Noon (أَلِف وَ نُوْن): Usually describing an internal state (عَطْشَانُ \- thirsty, غَضْبَانُ \- angry).  
2. The "Af’alu" Pattern (وَزْنُ أَفْعَلُ): Used for colors and superlatives (أَسْوَدُ \- black, أَكْبَرُ \- greater).  
3. Numerical Adjectives (الْعَدْلُ): Specific patterns for numbers 1–10 (مَثْنَى \- in twos, ثُلَاثُ \- in threes).

   ### **Module 3: Verb Classification (أَقْسَامُ الفِعْلِ)** {#module-3:-verb-classification-(أَقْسَامُ-الفِعْلِ)}

#### 3.1 Perspective: The "Relationship" {#3.1-perspective:-the-"relationship"}

Think of Perspective as the "camera angle" of a conversation. Depending on where the camera is pointed, the verbs and pronouns in Arabic change completely.

Here is the same action—sitting—viewed through the three different perspectives:

1\. The Speaker (1st Person: اَلْمُتَكَلِّمُ)  
The camera is on ME.

The Scenario: You are telling someone what you did.

The Example: "I sat on the chair."

Arabic Logic: You use pronouns like أَنَا (Ana \- I) or نَحْنُ (Nahnu \- We). In a verb like Jalasa (to sit), you add a specific ending to show you are the one talking: Jalas-tu.

2\. The Addressee (2nd Person: اَلْمُخَاطَبُ)  
The camera is on YOU.

The Scenario: You are looking at a friend and telling them what they did.

The Example: "You sat on the chair."

Arabic Logic: You must now choose based on who you are facing. If it's a male, you say أَنْتَ (Anta); if a female, أَنْتِ (Anti). The verb ending changes to match: Jalas-ta or Jalas-ti.

3\. The Absent (3rd Person: اَلْغَائِبُ)  
The camera is on HIM/HER (somewhere else).

The Scenario: you are gossiping or telling a story about someone who isn't part of the "Me and You" circle.

The Example: "He sat on the chair" or "She sat on the chair."

Arabic Logic: You use هُوَ (Huwa \- He) or هِيَ (Hiya \- She). The verb takes its simplest form: Jalasa (He sat) or Jalas-at (She sat).

#### 3.2 Tense {#3.2-tense}

Think of **Tense** as a **Timeline** that tells you where the action is located relative to the moment you are speaking.  You are looking behind you. The action is finished, closed, and set in stone. That’s Past.In Arabic. You are looking at what is happening right now that’s Present. You are looking ahead, That’s Future.

. The Past (اَلْمَاضِي \- *Al-Maadi*)

**The "Done" Deal.**

* "He **wrote** the letter." (كَتَبَ \- *Kataba*)  
* In Arabic, past tense verbs are "built" (*Mabni*), meaning their base endings don't change regardless of the sentence structure. It’s a solid fact of history.

2\. The Present (اَلْمُضَارِع \- *Al-Mudari'*)

**The "In-Motion" Action.**

* "He **is writing**" or "He **writes**." (يَكْتُبُ \- *Yaktubu*)  
* This is the only verb that can be in the states of **Raf’**, **Nasb**, or **Jazm**. It is alive and changing as the conversation flows.

3\. The Future (اَلْمُسْتَقْبَل \- *Al-Mustaqbal*)

**The "Coming Soon."**

* Arabic doesn't have a completely separate verb form for the future. Instead, you take the **Present Tense** and add a tiny "speed boost" to the front:  
  * **Add "Sa" (سَـ):** For the near future. (*Sayaktubu* \- He will write soon).  
  * **Add "Sawfa" (سَوْفَ):** For the distant future. (*Sawfa yaktubu* \- He will write eventually).

The 14 Conjugations (تَصْرِيفُ المَاضِي)  
Using the root **فَعَلَ** (He did) as our model:

| Perspective | Gender | Singular (1) | Dual (2) | Plural (3+) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Absent (3rd)** | **Masc.** | **فَعَلَ** (He) | **فَعَلَا** (They 2\) | **فَعَلُوا** (They) |
|  | **Fem.** | **فَعَلَتْ** (She) | **فَعَلَتَا** (They 2\) | **فَعَلْنَ** (They) |
| **Present (2nd)** | **Masc.** | **فَعَلْتَ** (You) | **فَعَلْتُمَا** (You 2\) | **فَعَلْتُمْ** (You all) |
|  | **Fem.** | **فَعَلْتِ** (You) | **فَعَلْتُمَا** (You 2\) | **فَعَلْتُنَّ** (You all) |

### 

Technical Observations (Key Rules)

1. **The Base Form:** The first form (**فَعَلَ**) has no visible suffix. The "He" is hidden inside.  
2. **The Feminine 'T':** The **تْ** at the end of **فَعَلَتْ** is just a sign that the subject is a lady. It has a **سُكُونٌ**.  
3. **The Alif of Protection:** In **فَعَلُوا**, the silent Alif at the end helps us distinguish the plural "Ooo" sound from other types of endings.  
4. **The Sukun Break:** Look closely at the 3rd letter of the root (the **ل**). From **فَعَلْنَ** (They women) all the way to the end of the table, that 3rd letter stays "still" with a **سُكُونٌ**.

#### **Lesson 3.4:** The Present Tense (الفِعْلُ المُضَارِعُ) {#lesson-3.4:-the-present-tense-(الفِعْلُ-المُضَارِعُ)}

In Arabic, the **Present Tense/ Future Tense (الفِعْلُ الْمُضَارِعُ)** is used to describe actions happening now or actions that happen habitually (like "I pray" or "He eats").

Unlike the Past Tense, which only changes at the **end** (suffixes), the Present Tense changes at the **beginning** and the **end**.

---

1\. The Four Signs (حُرُوفُ الْمُضَارَعَةِ)  
To turn a past verb into a present one, you must add one of four letters to the start. You can remember them by the word **أَنَيْتُ** (Anaytu) or **أَتَيْنَ** (Atayna):

* **أـ** : Used for "I" (**أَنَا**).  
* **نـ** : Used for "We" (**نَحْنُ**).  
* **يـ** : Used for "He/They" (**غَائِبٌ**).  
* **تـ** : Used for "She/You" (**مُخَاطَبٌ**).

---

2\. The 14 Conjugations (تَصْرِيفُ الْمُضَارِعِ)  
Using the root **فَعَلَ** (He did), let's see how it becomes **يَفْعُلُ** (He does).

*(Note: The middle vowel depends on the verb's "Bab" from Lesson 3.1. Here we use 'u').*

| Perspective | Gender | Singular (1) | Dual (2) | Plural (3+) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Absent (3rd)** | **Masc.** | **يَفْعُلُ** (He) | **يَفْعُلَانِ** (They 2\) | **يَفْعُلُونَ** (They) |
|  | **Fem.** | **تَفْعُلُ** (She) | **تَفْعُلَانِ** (They 2\) | **يَفْعُلْنَ** (They) |
| **Present (2nd)** | **Masc.** | **تَفْعُلُ** (You) | **تَفْعُلَانِ** (You 2\) | **تَفْعُلُونَ** (You all) |
|  | **Fem.** | **تَفْعُلِينَ** (You) | **تَفْعُلَانِ** (You 2\) | **تَفْعُلْنَ** (You all) |
| **Speaker (1st)** | **Both** | **أَفْعُلُ** (I) | — | **نَفْعُلُ** (We) |

---

3\. Key Observations

1. **The Starting Letter:** Notice that **يـ** is used for almost all the "Absent Masculine" forms, while **تـ** is used for all "Present/You" forms.  
2. **The "She" and "You" Match:** Notice that **تَفْعُلُ** can mean "She does" OR "You (man) do." We tell the difference based on the context of the sentence.  
3. **The Noon of Power (ن):** In many forms (like **يَفْعُلُونَ**), there is a **ن** at the end. In advanced lessons, we will learn that this **ن** can disappear if certain words come before the verb\!  
4. **The Future (الْمُسْتَقْبَلُ):** To make it absolute for future, we simply add a small prefix:  
   * **سَـ** (Soon): **سَيَفْعُلُ** (He will do soon).  
   * **سَوْفَ** (Later): **سَوْفَ يَفْعُلُ** (He will do later).

#### **Lesson 3.3:** Six Types of Verbs (أَنْوَاعُ الفِعْلِ السِّتَّة)  {#lesson-3.3:-six-types-of-verbs-(أَنْوَاعُ-الفِعْلِ-السِّتَّة)}

Think of the **Six Gates (أَبْوَاب)** as the "musical scales" of Arabic verbs. While the first and last letters of a three-letter verb usually stay consistent, the **middle letter (عَيْن)** acts like a volume knob that shifts between the Past and Present.

To master these, you just need to track how the vowel on the middle letter "tunes" itself when you move from the **Past** (مَاضِي) to the **Present** (مُضَارِع).

To understand the **Six Gates (أَبْوَاب)**, we look at the interaction between the **Past** and the **Present**.

There are only six possible combinations of vowels for this middle letter.

The Six Patterns (أَبْوَابُ الفِعْلِ)

Think of these as the six "formulas" for Arabic verbs.

| \# | Name of the Gate | Past Vowel | Present Vowel | The Change |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | **بَابُ نَصَرَ** | **ـَ** (Fat-ha) | **ـُ** (Damma) | $a \\rightarrow u$ |
| 2 | **بَابُ ضَرَبَ** | **ـَ** (Fat-ha) | **ـِ** (Kasra) | $a \\rightarrow i$ |
| 3 | **بَابُ فَتَحَ** | **ـَ** (Fat-ha) | **ـَ** (Fat-ha) | $a \\rightarrow a$ |
| 4 | **بَابُ سَمِعَ** | **ـِ** (Kasra) | **ـَ** (Fat-ha) | $i \\rightarrow a$ |
| 5 | **بَابُ كَرُمَ** | **ـُ** (Damma) | **ـُ** (Damma) | $u \\rightarrow u$ |
| 6 | **بَابُ حَسِبَ** | **ـِ** (Kasra) | **ـِ** (Kasra) | $i \\rightarrow i$ |

Key Technical Rules

* **The Dictionary Rule:** You cannot guess which gate a verb belongs to just by looking at the past tense. You must check a dictionary or hear it from a speaker. For example, **كَتَبَ** and **جَلَسَ** both have a fat-ha in the past, but one goes to Gate 1 (**يَكْتُبُ**) and the other to Gate 2 (**يَجْلِسُ**).  
* **The Throat Letter Rule (Gate 3):** For a verb to belong to **بَابُ فَتَحَ**, it usually has a "throat letter" (**ء، هـ، ع، ح، غ، خ**) as its second or third root letter.  
* From the first 3 all kind of verbs comes  
* **The Quality Rule (Gate 5):** Verbs in **بَابُ كَرُمَ** usually describe inherent qualities or nature, like being noble, beautiful, or small.  
* The Sensation Rule (Gate 4: بَابُ سَمِعَ) This gate is for **internal experiences**. It describes things that happen *to* you or inside you—feelings, learning, and temporary states of being.  
* 6\. The Rare used gate (Gate 6: بَابُ حَسِبَ) :  This is the rarest "club" in Arabic. There are very few verbs in this category.

#### **Lesson 8.3:** Active vs. Passive Voice (المَعْلُومُ وَالمَجْهُولُ) {#lesson-8.3:-active-vs.-passive-voice-(المَعْلُومُ-وَالمَجْهُولُ)}

We are now moving into the "Voice" of the verb. In Arabic, this is the difference between knowing exactly who did the work and focusing only on the work that was done.

---

1\. The Known (المَعْلُومُ \- Active Voice)  
This is the standard form of the verb where the **Doer (الفَاعِلُ)** is known or mentioned. The sentence follows the pattern we learned in Lesson 6.2.

* **Pattern:** Verb $\\rightarrow$ Known Doer $\\rightarrow$ Object  
* **Example:** **قَرَأَ الطَّالِبُ الكِتَابَ** (The student read the book).  
* **The Vibe:** Direct and clear.

---

2\. The Unknown (المَجْهُولُ \- Passive Voice)  
In this form, the **Doer is hidden, removed, or unknown**. The focus shifts entirely to the **Object**. When the doer is removed, the Object "steps up" to take the place of the Subject.

* **English Equivalent:** "The book **was read**." (We don't know or don't say who read it).  
* **Arabic Equivalent:** **قُرِئَ الكِتَابُ**

---

3\. The Grammar Shift: How to Build the Passive  
To change a verb from Active to Passive, we don't add new letters; we change the **vowels (Harakat)**. This acts as a "code" to tell the listener that the doer is missing.

A. The Past Tense (المَاضِي)

* **Rule:** Damma on the first letter, Kasra on the second-to-last letter.  
* **Active:** **فَعَلَ** ($Fa-'a-la$)  
* **Passive:** **فُعِلَ** ($Fu-'i-la$)  
* **Example:** **كَتَبَ** (He wrote) $\\rightarrow$ **كُتِبَ** (It was written).

B. The Present Tense (المُضَارِعُ)

* **Rule:** Damma on the first letter (the prefix), Fat-ha on the second-to-last letter.  
* **Active:** **يَفْعَلُ** ($Yaf-'a-lu$)  
* **Passive:** **يُفْعَلُ** ($Yuf-'a-lu$)  
* **Example:** **يَقْتُلُ** (He kills) $\\rightarrow$ **يُقْتَلُ** (He is being killed).

---

4\. The "Deputy Subject" (نَائِبُ الفَاعِلِ)  
When the Doer (**الفَاعِلُ**) leaves the sentence, the Object takes its place.

1. The Object moves from the **Mansoob** state (Fat-ha) to the **Marfoo'** state (Damma).  
2. It is no longer called the "Object"; it is now called the **Deputy Subject (نَائِبُ الفَاعِلِ)**.

> **Active:** **خَلَقَ اللهُ الإِنْسَانَ** (Allah created mankind).

> **Passive:** **خُلِقَ الإِنْسَانُ** (Mankind was created).

#### **Lesson 3.4:** The Command (فِعْلُ الأَمْرِ) {#lesson-3.4:-the-command-(فِعْلُ-الأَمْرِ)}

The **Command Verb (فِعْلُ الأَمْرِ)** is used to request or order an action. In Arabic, you only give a direct command to someone standing in front of you—the **Present/Addressed Perspective (المُخَاطَب)**.

Because of this, there are only **6 patterns** for the command verb (Singular, Dual, and Plural for Masculine and Feminine).

---

How to Build a Command from the Present Tense (المُضَارِع)  
Creating a command is like a 4-step surgical process. Let's use the verb **تَنْصُرُ** (You help) as our patient.

**Step 1: Start with the 2nd Person Present Tense**

Take the "You" form of the verb.

> Example: **تَنْصُرُ** (You help)

**Step 2: Remove the "You" Prefix (تـ)**

Cut off the present tense sign at the beginning.

> Result: **ـنْصُرُ**

**Step 3: Make the Ending "Still" (Jazm)**

If the word ends in a Damma, change it to a **سُكُون**. If it ends in a **ن**, drop the **ن** (except for the feminine plural).

> Result: **ـنْصُرْ**

**Step 4: Add a "Helper Alif" (ا) if needed**

Look at the first letter after you removed the **تـ**. If it has a **سُكُون** (like the **نْ** in our example), you can't pronounce it\! You must add a "bridge" Alif.

* **Which vowel for the Alif?**  
  * Look at the middle letter. If it has a **ضَمَّة**, the Alif gets a **ضَمَّة** $\\rightarrow$ (**اُنْصُرْ**).  
  * If the middle letter has a **فَتْحَة** or **كَسْرَة**, the Alif gets a **كَسْرَة** $\\rightarrow$ (**اِضْرِبْ** / **اِفْتَحْ**).

---

The 6 Patterns of the Command (تَصْرِيفُ الأَمْرِ)  
Using the root **نَصَرَ** (To help) through its Gate (**يَنْصُرُ**):

| Gender | Singular (1) | Dual (2) | Plural (3+) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Masculine** | **اُنْصُرْ** (Help\!) | **اُنْصُرَا** (Help you 2\!) | **اُنْصُرُوا** (Help you all\!) |
| **Feminine** | **اُنْصُرِي** (Help\!) | **اُنْصُرَا** (Help you 2\!) | **اُنْصُرْنَ** (Help you all\!) |

#### **Lesson 3.5:** The Prohibitive (فِعْلُ النَّهْيِ) {#lesson-3.5:-the-prohibitive-(فِعْلُ-النَّهْيِ)}

**Nahi (النَّهْي)** is the grammatical term for **Prohibition**. While the *Amr* (Command) tells someone *to do* something, the *Nahi* tells them **"Do not do it."**

Think of it as the "negative partner" of the command. In Arabic, this is achieved by using a specific type of "No" called **لَا النَّاهِيَة** (The *Laa* of Prohibition).

---

How to Construct the Nahi  
Unlike the Command (*Amr*), you don't have to cut the verb apart or add extra "helper" letters. You simply take the Present Tense verb and apply two changes:

1. **Add the Stop Sign:** Place **لَا** (Laa) before the **Present Tense** (2nd person).  
2. **Make it "Still" (Jazm):** The verb ending must change to the "Jazm" state.  
   * **Single:** The Damma becomes a **Sukun** (**ـُ** $\\rightarrow$ **ـْ**).  
   * **Dual/Plural:** The **Noon (ن)** at the end is dropped.  
   * **Exception:** The **Noon** in the feminine plural (**تَفْعَلْنَ**) stays—it's "stuck" and never leaves.

---

The 6 Patterns of Prohibition (المُخَاطَب)  
Using the root **فَعَلَ / يَفْعُلُ** (To do):

| Gender | Singular (1) | Dual (2) | Plural (3+) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Masculine** | **لَا تَفْعُلْ** (Don't do\!) | **لَا تَفْعُلَا** (Don't you 2 do\!) | **لَا تَفْعُلُوا** (Don't you all do\!) |
| **Feminine** | **لَا تَفْعُلِي** (Don't do\!) | **لَا تَفْعُلَا** (Don't you 2 do\!) | **لَا تَفْعُلْنَ** (Don't you all do\!) |

---

Important Distinction: "No" vs "Don't"  
A common mistake is confusing **Nahi** (Prohibition) with **Nafi** (Negation).

* **Negation (Nafi):** Just a statement of fact. "You **don't** help."  
  * Arabic: **لَا تَنْصُرُ** (The verb stays normal with a Damma).  
* **Prohibition (Nahi):** An order. "**Don't** help\!"  
  * Arabic: **لَا تَنْصُرْ** (The verb ends in a **Sukun**).

  ### **Module 4: Particles (الحُرُوفُ)** {#module-4:-particles-(الحُرُوفُ)}

Welcome to Module 4, where we move away from the "Action" (Verbs) and the "Things" (Nouns) and look at the **"Glue"** of the language: الحَرْفُ (The Particle).

What is a Harf (الحَرْفُ)?  
In Arabic grammar, a Harf is defined as a word that does not have a complete meaning on its own.

* If I say "Book" (كِتَابٌ), you picture a book.  
* If I say "He wrote" (كَتَبَ), you picture an action.  
* But if I say "From" (مِنْ) or "In" (فِي), you are left waiting for the next word.

A Harf only shows its true meaning when it is joined with a Noun or a Verb. Its job is to link words together to create logic, direction, or emphasis.

Key Characteristic: اَلْمَبْنِيُّ (The Unchangeable)  
The most important thing to remember about حُرُوف (Particles) is that they are all مَبْنِيٌّ.

* They never change their "outfits."  
* مِنْ will always be مِنْ, regardless of where it sits in a sentence. They are the most stable parts of the Arabic language.

Overview: Types of Harf  
There are many different families of Particles. Each family has a specific "power" over the words that follow them. Here is a brief look at the most common types:

1\. The Pullers (حُرُوفُ الجَرِّ \- Prepositions)

These are the most famous. They sit before a Noun and "pull" its ending down into a كَسْرَةٌ (the state of جَرٌّ).

* *Examples:* مِنْ (From), فِي (In), إِلَى (To).

2\. The Linkers (حُرُوفُ العَطْفِ \- Conjunctions)

These join two words or two sentences together.

* *Examples:* وَ (And), فَـ (Then), أَوْ (Or).

3\. The Stressor (إِنَّ وَأَخَوَاتُهَا \- Emphasis Particle)

These enter upon a sentence to add emphasis or "certainty." They change the vowel of the noun that follows them to a فَتْحَةٌ.

* *Examples:* إِنَّ (Indeed), لَكِنَّ (But).

4\. The Questioners (أَدَوَاتُ الِاسْتِفْهَامِ \- Interrogative Particles)

These are used at the start of a sentence to turn it into a question.

* *Examples:* هَلْ (Is/Do/Are?), أَ (Is it?).

5\. The Vowel-Changers for Verbs (النَّوَاصِب وَالجَوَازِم)

Some particles only interact with Present Tense Verbs. Some make the verb end in a فَتْحَةٌ (like لَنْ \- "Will never"), and others make it end in a سُكُونٌ (like لَمْ \- "Did not").

6\. The Callers (حُرُوفُ النِّدَاءِ \- Vocative Particles)

Used to call someone's attention.

* *Example:* يَا (O... / Hey...).

We will look at all huruf later, for now we focus on 2 types

#### **Lesson 4.1:** Particles of Jarr (حُرُوفُ الجَرِّ) {#lesson-4.1:-particles-of-jarr-(حُرُوفُ-الجَرِّ)}

The **Particles of Jarr (حُرُوفُ الجَرِّ)**, often called "Prepositions," are a specific group of words that only enter upon **Nouns**.

Their primary job is to connect nouns to other parts of the sentence, but they have a "superpower": they change the grammatical state of the noun that follows them.

---

1\. The Rule of the Harf Jarr  
When a Harf Jarr appears before a noun, that noun must enter the state of **جَرّ (Jar)**.

* **The Sign:** For most singular nouns, the sign of Jar is a **Kasra (—ِ)** on the last letter.  
* **The Term:** The noun after a Harf Jarr is called a **مَجْرُور (Majroor)**.

> **Formula:** Harf Jarr \+ Noun \= Noun with a Kasra.

> **Example:** **البَيْتُ** (The house) $\\rightarrow$ **فِي البَيْتِ** (In the house).

---

2\. Common Particles of Jarr  
There are 17 in total, but these 9 are the most frequently used in the Quran and daily Arabic:

| Harf Jarr | Meaning | Example | Translation |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **مِنْ** | From | **مِنَ الْمَسْجِدِ** | From the Masjid |
| **إِلَى** | To / Towards | **إِلَى الْمَدْرَسَةِ** | To the school |
| **عَنْ** | About / Away from | **عَنِ النَّبِيِّ** | About the Prophet |
| **عَلَى** | On / Upon | **عَلَى الْمَكْتَبِ** | On the desk |
| **فِي** | In / Inside | **فِي الْقَلْبِ** | In the heart |
| **بِـ** | With / By | **بِالْقَلَمِ** | With the pen |
| **لِـ** | For / Belonging to | **لِلَّهِ** | For Allah |
| **كَـ** | Like / As | **كَالْأَسَدِ** | Like the lion |
| **حَتَّى** | Until | **حَتَّى الْفَجْرِ** | Until dawn |

---

3\. Special Formatting Rules

* **بِـ / لِـ / كَـ**: These are single letters that "stick" directly to the beginning of the noun.  
* **لِـ (For)**: When this sticks to a word with **الـ**, the Alif of the "Al" is dropped.  
  * Example: **الْحَمْدُ** \+ **لِـ** \= **لِلَّهِ** (not لِالْحَمْدُ).  
* **مِنْ / عَنْ**: If the word after them starts with a Sukun (like **الـ**), they take a small vowel to help the pronunciation.  
  * **مِنْ** becomes **مِنَ** (**مِنَ اللهِ**).  
  * **عَنْ** becomes **عَنِ** (**عَنِ النَّبِيِّ**).

#### **Lesson 4.2:** The Conjunction (حُرُوفُ العَطْفِ) {#lesson-4.2:-the-conjunction-(حُرُوفُ-العَطْفِ)}

Particles of Conjunction (حُرُوفُ العَطْفِ) are the "linkers" of the Arabic language. Their job is to join two words or two sentences together.

The word that comes before the conjunction is called the المَعْطُوف عَلَيْهِ (The followed), and the word that comes after it is called the المَعْطُوف (The follower).

1\. The Mirror Rule (The Power of Coordination)

Conjunctions are like mirrors. The word *after* the conjunction must copy the grammatical state (the ending vowel) of the word *before* it.

* If the first word has a Damma, the second word gets a Damma.  
* If the first word has a Kasra, the second word gets a Kasra.

> Example: جَاءَ زَيْدٌ وَعَمْرٌو (Zayd and 'Amr came).

> Amr copied the damma of zaid because of mirror Waw..

---

2\. Common Particles of Conjunction

Each conjunction adds a slightly different meaning to the connection:

| Harf Atf | Meaning | Nuance / Timing |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| وَ | And | Simple joining (no specific order/time). |
| فَـ | Then / So | Immediate succession (Right after). |
| ثُمَّ | Then | Succession with a delay (After a while). |
| أَوْ | Or | Choice between two things. |
| بَلْ | But / Rather | Correcting or adding to a previous thought. |
| أَمْ | Or | Used specifically in questions. |

---

3\. Deep Dive into the "Then" Duo (فَـ vs ثُمَّ)

This is a very important distinction in Arabic logic, especially in the Quran:

* فَـ (Immediate): دَخَلَ زَيْدٌ فَـعَمْرٌو $\\rightarrow$ Zayd entered, and *immediately* behind him, 'Amr entered.  
* ثُمَّ (Delayed): دَخَلَ زَيْدٌ ثُمَّ عَمْرٌو $\\rightarrow$ Zayd entered, and *after some time* (maybe an hour), 'Amr entered.

  ### **Module 5: Phrases (المُرَكَّبَاتُ النَّاقِصَةُ)** {#module-5:-phrases-(المُرَكَّبَاتُ-النَّاقِصَةُ)}

Are you ready to build something bigger? Up until now, we have been looking at single words—nouns, verbs, and particles—like individual bricks. But bricks alone don't make a wall. We need to join them together. 

A **مُرَكَّبٌ نَاقِصٌ** (Fragment) is a combination of two or more words that gives us some meaning, but not a full sentence. If I say "The Messenger's book," you are still waiting for me to finish the thought. It is a "fragment" because it is incomplete on its own.

There are 3 main types of fragments we will master:

1. **المُرَكَّبُ الإِضَافِيُّ** (Possessive Fragment)  
2. **المُرَكَّبُ الوَصْفِيُّ** (Descriptive Fragment)  
3. **المُرَكَّبُ الإِشَارِيُّ** (Pointing Fragment)

#### **Lesson 5.1:** The Possessive Phrase (الإِضَافَةُ) {#lesson-5.1:-the-possessive-phrase-(الإِضَافَةُ)}

Imagine you have two separate nouns—like **"Book"** and **"Allah"**—floating in space. On their own, they are just independent words. But in Arabic, we can "glue" them together to create a powerful connection of belonging.

This "Grammatical Glue" is called **الإِضَافَةُ (The Idafah)**.

* **بَيْتُ اللهِ**  
* **قَلَمُ الطَّالِبِ**  
* **دِينُ الإِسْلَامِ**

In English, we say this in  2 ways:

* "The teacher's car" (Adding “s”)  
* "The door of the masjid" (adding “of”)

The only difference between them is order is opposite in both language 

The **مُرَكَّبٌ إِضَافِيٌّ** (Possessive Fragment) consists of two parts:

1. **المُضَافُ**: The thing being owned \- It’s a minimalist. It drops its *feather*,  light and ready to be connected.  
2. **المُضَافُ إِلَيْهِ**: The owner (The "Owner")-  It is the "Boss" of the relationship.

---

Rules

1. **Rules for the المُضَافُ (The Owned):**  
   * It **cannot** have **الـ** at the beginning.  
   * It **cannot** have **تَنْوِينٌ** (double vowels) at the end. It must have a single vowel (**ـُ / ـَ / ـِ**).  
2. **Rules for the المُضَافُ إِلَيْهِ (The Owner):**  
   * It must **always** be in the state of **جَرٌّ**. This means its default sign is a **كَسْرَةٌ**.

Quick \- a mudaf can have **الـ** if it’s a derived from masdar and acting like verb

#### **Lesson 5.2:** Adjective-Noun Agreement (النَّعْتُ وَالمَنْعُوتُ) {#lesson-5.2:-adjective-noun-agreement-(النَّعْتُ-وَالمَنْعُوتُ)}

Imagine you are standing at a fruit stall. You see two crates of apples: one has **Red Apples** and the other has **Green Apples**.

In this scenario, the **Apple** is the noun (**Man'ut**), and **Red** or **Green** is the adjective (**Na't**).

Why do we need both?

* You need the **Man'ut** (Apple) to know **what** you are talking about.  
* You need the **Na't** (Red/Green) to know **which** specific one you want.

**تُفَّاحٌ أَحْمَرُ** 

* **English:** A red apple  
* **Rule:** Both are masculine, singular, and end in the same "U" sound.

**تُفَّاحَةٌ خَضْرَاء**

* **English:** A green apple  
* **Rule:** Both are feminine (the apple has a **ة**, so the color becomes feminine).

**اَلتُّفَّاحُ الْحُلْوُ** 

* **English:** The sweet apple  
* **Rule:** Both are wearing the **ال** (The) "uniform."

**تُفَّاحَتَانِ كَبِيْرَتَانِ** 

* **English:** Two big apples  
* **Rule:** Both are dual (they both end in **انِ** to show there are two).

**فِي سُوقٍ نَظِيفٍ** 

* **English:** In a clean market  
* **Rule:** Both have a *Kasra* (I-sound) at the end because they follow the word "In."

**تُفَّاحٌ صَغِيرٌ**

* **English:** Small apples  
* **Rule:** Both are indefinite (no **ال**) and share the same "U" ending.

The "Price Tag" Connection

In Arabic, these two are glued together like a price tag on a fruit. The tag (**Na't**) must follow the fruit (**Man'ut**) wherever it goes. This means they must match perfectly in four ways:

1. **Gender:** If the Apple is feminine, the Color must be feminine.  
2. **Number:** If you have two Apples, you must have two "Reds."  
3. **Definiteness:** If the Apple has "**Al-**" (The), the Color must also have "**Al-**."  
4. **Grammatical Case (Irab):** If the Apple ends with a *Damma*, the Color must also end with a *Damma*.

In English, we say:

* "A small book"  
* "The tall teacher"

Rules

To be a valid "Shadow" relationship, the **النَّعْتُ** (Adjective) must follow the **الْمَنْعُوتُ** (Noun) in **4 things**:

1. **State (إِعْرَابٌ):** If the noun is **رَفْعٌ** (Damma), the adjective must be **رَفْعٌ**. If it is **جَرٌّ** (Kasra), the adjective is **جَرٌّ**.  
2. **Gender (جِنْسٌ):** Masculine noun gets a masculine adjective. Feminine noun gets a feminine adjective (usually with a **ة**).  
3. **Capacity (وُسْعَةٌ):** If the noun has **الـ** (Definite), the adjective **must** have **الـ**. If the noun is **نَكِرَةٌ** (Indefinite/Tanween), the adjective must have **تَنْوِينٌ**.  
4. **Number (عَدَدٌ):** Singular follows singular, dual follows dual, and plural follows plural.

#### **Lesson 5.3:** Demonstrative Phrases (أَسْمَاءُ الإِشَارَةِ) {#lesson-5.3:-demonstrative-phrases-(أَسْمَاءُ-الإِشَارَةِ)}

Have you ever noticed how often we point at things while talking? "Look at **this** book" or "Pass me **that** pen." In Arabic, these pointing words are called **أَسْمَاءُ الإِشَارَةِ** (Demonstrative Nouns).

* **هَذَا الكِتَابُ**  
* **ذَلِكَ الرَّجُلُ**  
* **تِلْكَ الشَّجَرَةُ**

In English, we have:

* "This student" (Near)  
* "That star" (Far)

The **مُرَكَّبٌ إِشَارِيٌّ** consists of two parts:

1. **اِسْمُ الإِشَارَةِ**: The pointing word (This/That).  
2. **المُشَارُ إِلَيْهِ**: The object being pointed at.

Rules

1. **The "AL" Rule:** To make a fragment (like "This book"), the object **must** have **الـ**. If you say **هَذَا كِتَابٌ** (without **الـ**), it becomes a full sentence: "This is a book."  
2. **Distance:** Arabic distinguishes between "Near" (this) and "Far" (that).  
3. **Agreement:** The pointing word must match the object in **Gender** (Masculine/Feminine) and **Number** (Singular/Dual/Plural).  
4. **Fixed State:** Most pointing words are **مَبْنِيٌّ** (unchanging). For example, **هَذَا** stays **هَذَا** whether it is in a state of Raf, Nasb, or Jarr

| Number | Near (Masc) | Near (Fem) | Far (Masc) | Far (Fem) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Singular (1)** | **هَذَا** | **هَذِهِ** | **ذَلِكَ** | **تِلْكَ** |
| **Dual (2)** | **هَذَانِ** | **هَاتَانِ** | **ذَانِكَ** | **تَانِكَ** |
| **Plural (3+)** | **هَؤُلَاءِ** | **هَؤُلَاءِ** | **أُولَئِكَ** | **أُولَئِكَ** |

   ### **Module 6: The Sentence Core (أَرْكَانُ الجُمْلَةِ)** {#module-6:-the-sentence-core-(أَرْكَانُ-الجُمْلَةِ)}

We have reached the most exciting part of our journey\! We have mastered the **Bricks** (Single Words) and the **Combined Blocks** (Fragments). Now, we are ready to build the **Wall**: **الْجُمْلَةُ** (The Sentence).

In Arabic, a sentence is a combination of words that gives a complete, perfect meaning. When you finish speaking, the listener is not waiting for more information—they have received a full news report.

---

* **اللهُ أَكْبَرُ** (Allah is the Greatest)  
* **جَاءَ النَّصْرُ** (The victory came)  
* **الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ** (All praise is for Allah)

Now there are 3 different kind of sentences in arabic.

#### **Lesson 6.1:** Nominal Sentence (الجُمْلَةُ الاِسْمِيَّةُ): Subject & Predicate (المُبْتَدَأُ وَالخَبَرُ). {#lesson-6.1:-nominal-sentence-(الجُمْلَةُ-الاِسْمِيَّةُ):-subject-&-predicate-(المُبْتَدَأُ-وَالخَبَرُ).}

Welcome to the first type of "Wall" we will build. The **Nominal Sentence (الجُمْلَةُ الاِسْمِيَّةُ)** is the simplest way to make a statement in Arabic. It is a sentence that **starts with a Noun**.

If you want to say "The house is big" or "Allah is Merciful," you are building a Nominal Sentence.

The Two Pillars

A Nominal Sentence is built on two main pillars. If one is missing, the wall collapses.

1. **المُبْتَدَأُ (The Subject):** The noun you are starting with. It is the "Topic" of your sentence. It usually comes first.  
2. **الخَبَرُ (The Predicate):** The "News" or information you are giving about the Subject. It completes the meaning.

In English, we need the word "is," "am," or "are" to link a subject and a predicate (e.g., "The water **is** cold").

In Arabic, **there is no word for "is."** When you place a specific type of Subject next to a specific type of Predicate, the "is" appears automatically in the meaning.

---

Rules

To make the "is" appear and the sentence be correct, follow these three golden rules:

1. **The State (Case):** Both the **المُبْتَدَأُ** and the **الخَبَرُ** must be in the state of **رَفْعٌ** (usually ending with a **Damma** or **Tanween Damma**).  
2. **The Capacity (Definiteness):**  
   * The **المُبْتَدَأُ** (Subject) is usually **Definite** (starts with **الـ**).  
   * The **الخَبَرُ** (Predicate) is usually **Indefinite** (ends with **Tanween**).  
3. **Gender Agreement:** If the Subject is Masculine, the Predicate must be Masculine. If the Subject is Feminine, the Predicate must be Feminine.

> **Example:** **البَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ** (The house \[is\] big).

> * **البَيْتُ**: Definite \+ Damma (**Mubtada**)  
> * **كَبِيرٌ**: Indefinite \+ Tanween Damma (**Khabar**)

Advance rules : 

#### **Lesson 6.2:** Verbal Sentence (الجُمْلَةُ الفِعْلِيَّةُ): The Subject (الفَاعِلُ) and The Object (المَفْعُولُ بِهِ). {#lesson-6.2:-verbal-sentence-(الجُمْلَةُ-الفِعْلِيَّةُ):-the-subject-(الفَاعِلُ)-and-the-object-(المَفْعُولُ-بِهِ).}

Now that we have built the Nominal Wall, it is time to build the **Verbal Sentence (الجُمْلَةُ الفِعْلِيَّةُ)**. This "Wall" is all about action. It describes something happening, who did it, and what they did it to.

A Verbal Sentence is any sentence that **starts with a Verb**.

The Three Pillars  
A complete Verbal Sentence can have up to three main parts:

1. **الفِعْلُ (The Verb):** The action (Past, Present, or Command). This must come first.  
2. **الفَاعِلُ (The Subject/Doer):** The one who performed the action.  
3. **المَفْعُولُ بِهِ (The Object):** The one or thing that received the action. (Note: Not every verb needs an object).

In English, we say: "The boy (Doer) ate (Action) the apple (Object)."  
In Arabic, the order is usually: "Ate (Action) the boy (Doer) the apple (Object)."

* English: Subject $\\rightarrow$ Verb $\\rightarrow$ Object  
* Arabic: **Verb $\\rightarrow$ Subject $\\rightarrow$ Object**

Rules

To distinguish between the person "doing" and the thing "receiving," Arabic uses vowels as labels:

1. **The Doer (الفَاعِلُ):** Must always be in the state of **رَفْعٌ** (having sign of rafa ,usually ends with a **Damma** or **Tanween Damma**).  
2. **The Object (المَفْعُولُ بِهِ):** Must always be in the state of **نَصْبٌ** (having a sign of nasab, usually ends with a **Fat-ha** or **Tanween Fat-ha**).  
3. **Gender Match:** The verb must match the **Doer** in gender. If the boy eats, use **أَكَلَ**. If the girl eats, use **أَكَلَتْ**.

> **Example:** **قَرَأَ الطَّالِبُ الكِتَابَ** (The student read the book).

> * **قَرَأَ**: Verb (Past)  
> * **الطَّالِبُ**: Ends with Damma (**Doer**)  
> * **الكِتَابَ**: Ends with Fat-ha (**Object**)

Advance : 

#### **Lesson 6.3:** The Inshaiyyah Sentence (الجُمْلَةُ الإِنْشَائِيَّةُ) {#lesson-6.3:-the-inshaiyyah-sentence-(الجُمْلَةُ-الإِنْشَائِيَّةُ)}

Up until now, we have studied the **Khabariyyah Sentence (الجُمْلَةُ الْخَبَرِيَّةُ)**—sentences that give us a "news report" or a statement of fact that could be true or false (e.g., "The house is big" or "Zayd went").

But what if you aren't giving news? What if you are asking a question, giving an order, or making a wish? This is where we use the **Inshaiyyah Sentence (الجُمْلَةُ الإِنْشَائِيَّةُ)**. This type of sentence is one where you cannot call the speaker a "liar" or "truth-teller" because they aren't reporting a fact; they are **creating** a request, a feeling, or a demand.

* **هَلْ شَرِبْتَ الْمَاءَ؟** (Did you drink the water?)  
* **اِجْلِسْ هُنَا\!** (Sit here\!)  
* **لَا تَحْزَنْ\!** (Do not be sad\!)  
* **يَا اللهُ\!** (O Allah\!)

A **جُمْلَةٌ إِنْشَائِيَّةٌ** is a sentence that establishes a meaning that did not exist before the words were spoken. It is not "news"; it is an interaction.

Rules

1. **Non-Truth Bearing:** You cannot say "That is true" or "That is a lie" to an Inshaiyyah sentence. If I say "Sit down\!", it is an order, not a statement of fact.  
2. **Types of Inshaiyyah:** There are many types, but the most common are:  
   * **الأَمْرُ (Command):** Ordering something to happen.  
   * **النَّهْيُ (Prohibition):** Ordering something to stop.  
   * **الِاسْتِفْهَامُ (Question):** Asking for information.  
   * **النِّدَاءُ (Calling):** Calling someone's attention (using **يَا**).  
   * **التَّمَنِّي (Wishing):** Expressing a wish (using **لَيْتَ**).

### Module 7: The Change-Agents (النَّوَاسِخُ) {#module-7:-the-change-agents-(النَّوَاسِخُ)}

#### **Lesson 7.1:** *Kāna* and its Sisters (كَانَ وَأَخَوَاتُهَا) {#lesson-7.1:-kāna-and-its-sisters-(كَانَ-وَأَخَوَاتُهَا)}

Welcome to **Module 7**\! You have mastered the "Wall" (the basic sentence), but now we are going to introduce **Change-Agents (النَّوَاسِخُ)**. These are words that enter a perfectly stable sentence and change its rules.

Think of them as "mood ring" words. They change the atmosphere and the grammar of the sentence they enter. We start with the most famous group: **كَانَ وَأَخَوَاتُهَا** (Kana and its Sisters).

---

The Function of Kāna

Originally, a Nominal Sentence (**جُمْلَةٌ اسْمِيَّةٌ**) has two pillars that are both in the state of **رَفْعٌ** (Damma).

* Example: **زَيْدٌ قَائِمٌ** (Zayd is standing).

When **كَانَ** (Was) enters the sentence, it changes the "News" (the Predicate) but leaves the "Subject" alone.

---

Rules

1. **The Subject (اِسْمُ كَانَ):** Remains in the state of **رَفْعٌ** (Damma).  
2. **The Predicate (خَبَرُ كَانَ):** Changes to the state of **نَصْبٌ** (Fat-ha).  
3. **The Sisters:** These are other verbs that act exactly like **كَانَ** but have different meanings.

| Sister | Meaning | Example |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **كَانَ** | Was | **كَانَ اللهُ غَفُورًا** (Allah was/is All-Forgiving) |
| **أَصْبَحَ** | Became (in the morning) | **أَصْبَحَ المَاءُ بَارِدًا** (The water became cold) |
| **صَارَ** | Became / Transformed | **صَارَ الطِّينُ خَزَفًا** (The clay became pottery) |
| **لَيْسَ** | Is not (Negation) | **لَيْسَ النَّجَاحُ سَهْلًا** (Success is not easy) |
| **مَا زَالَ** | Still | **مَا زَالَ الطَّالِبُ نَائِمًا** (The student is still sleeping) |

#### **Lesson 7.2:** *Inna* and its Sisters (إِنَّ وَأَخَوَاتُهَا) {#lesson-7.2:-inna-and-its-sisters-(إِنَّ-وَأَخَوَاتُهَا)}

If \*\*كَانَ\*\* was the "Gentle Giant" that changed the end of the sentence, then \*\*إِنَّ\*\* (Inna) and its sisters are the "Opposite Force."

While \*\*كَانَ\*\* leaves the Subject alone and changes the Predicate, \*\*إِنَّ\*\* does the exact opposite: it attacks the \*\*Subject\*\* and leaves the \*\*Predicate\*\* alone.

\#\#\# The Function of Inna

The primary job of \*\*إِنَّ\*\* is \*\*تَوْكِيدٌ\*\* (Emphasis/Certainty). It turns a simple statement into a powerful fact.

\*   Normal: \*\*اللهُ غَفُورٌ\*\* (Allah is Forgiving).

\*   With Inna: \*\*إِنَّ اللهَ غَفُورٌ\*\* (Indeed, Allah is Forgiving).

\#\#\# Rules

1\.  \*\*The Subject (اِسْمُ إِنَّ):\*\* Changes to the state of \*\*نَصْبٌ\*\* (Fat-ha).

2\.  \*\*The Predicate (خَبَرُ إِنَّ):\*\* Remains in its original state of \*\*رَفْعٌ\*\* (Damma).

3\.  \*\*The Purpose:\*\* Most of these sisters are used to add a layer of meaning like "Emphasis," "Wishing," or "Likening."

| Sister | Meaning | Example |

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| \*\*إِنَّ\*\* | Indeed / Verily | \*\*إِنَّ اللهَ مَعَنَا\*\* (Indeed Allah is with us) |

| \*\*أَنَّ\*\* | That | \*\*أَعْلَمُ أَنَّ اللهَ قَدِيرٌ\*\* (I know that Allah is powerful) |

| \*\*كَأَنَّ\*\* | As if / Like | \*\*كَأَنَّ العِلْمَ نُورٌ\*\* (As if knowledge is light) |

| \*\*لَكِنَّ\*\* | But / However | \*\*الكِتَابُ صَغِيرٌ لَكِنَّ العِلْمَ كَبِيرٌ\*\* (The book is small but the knowledge is great) |

| \*\*لَيْتَ\*\* | If only (Wishing) | \*\*لَيْتَ الشَّبَابَ يَعُودُ\*\* (If only youth would return) |

| \*\*لَعَلَّ\*\* | Perhaps / So that | \*\*لَعَلَّ اللهَ يُحْدِثُ أَمْرًا\*\* (Perhaps Allah will bring about a matter) |

## **🗺️ LEVEL 2: INTERMEDIATE SYNTHESIS (التَّرْكِيبُ المُتَوَسِّطُ)** {#🗺️-level-2:-intermediate-synthesis-(التَّرْكِيبُ-المُتَوَسِّطُ)}

### **Module 8: Intermediate Morphology (تَصْرِيفُ الأَفْعَالِ)** {#module-8:-intermediate-morphology-(تَصْرِيفُ-الأَفْعَالِ)}

#### **Lesson 8.1:** 3-Letter vs. Expanded Roots (الثُّلَاثِيُّ وَالمَزِيدُ) {#lesson-8.1:-3-letter-vs.-expanded-roots-(الثُّلَاثِيُّ-وَالمَزِيدُ)}

1\. The Core Concept (DNA vs. Growth)  
Every Arabic word has a **Root** (the DNA).

* **Bare (3-Letter):** This is the DNA in its simplest, most natural state. Like k ata ba  
* **Expanded (Mazid):** This is when you "engineer" the DNA by adding letters to create a specific function or result. eg….

	   
**2\. The Functional "Why" (The Purpose of Expanding)**  
In Arabic, we don't just add letters for sound; we add them for **Logic**. Here are the three main "Why" categories:

A. The Power Up (Intensity)

When you double a letter (using a Shadda), you are physically making the word "heavier."

* **Bare:** **قَطَعَ** (He cut) $\\rightarrow$ Simple action.  
* **Expanded:** **قَطَّعَ** (He chopped/shredded) $\\rightarrow$ The extra letter means the action was done many times or with great force.

B. The "Push" (Making it Happen to Others)

Sometimes you add a letter to turn an action into an influence.

* **Bare:** **نَزَلَ** (He descended/came down) $\\rightarrow$ He did it himself.  
* **Expanded:** **أَنْزَلَ** (He **sent down**) $\\rightarrow$ The "Alif" at the start acts like a push; he made something else come down (like the Rain or the Quran).

C. The "Request" (Seeking)

Adding the specific prefix **اِسْتَـ** (Ist) tells the listener that the doer is *looking* for something.

* **Bare:** **غَفَرَ** (He forgave).  
* **Expanded:** **اِسْتَغْفَرَ** (He **sought** forgiveness) $\\rightarrow$ The added letters mean he is asking for the root action to happen.

D. The "Transitive" Bridge (Tadiya \- التَّعْدِيَة)

* **The Bare Verb (Lāzim):** The action is "lazy" and stays at home with the doer.  
  * **خَرَجَ** (*Kharaja* \- He went out). He just moved himself.  
* **The Expanded Verb (Muta'addī):** By adding an "Alif" at the beginning (Form IV) or doubling the middle letter (Form II), you force the action to hit a target.  
  * **أَخْرَجَ** (*Akhraja* \- He **brought out/removed** something). Now there is an object involved, like a key from a pocket.

3\. Comparison of Meaning "Flavors"

| Root DNA | Bare (Pure) | Expanded (Mazid) | The "Why" Change |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **ع \- ل \- م** | **عَلِمَ** (He knew) | **عَلَّمَ** (He taught) | Added Shadda: Made *someone else* know. |
| **ع \- ل \- م** | **عَلِمَ** (He knew) | **تَعَلَّمَ** (He learned) | Added Ta: Put in the *effort* to know. |
| **ق \- ر \- ب** | **قَرُبَ** (He was near) | **قَرَّبَ** (He brought near) | Added Shadda: Forced the proximity. |
| **ق \- ر \- ب** | **قَرُبَ** (He was near) | **اِسْتَقْرَبَ** (He considered it near) | Added Ist: His *perception* sought nearness. |

In Arabic morphology, the expanded roots (**الثُّلَاثِيُّ الْمَزِيدُ فِيهِ**) are organized into logical "Filing Cabinets" called **Abwāb** (singular: *Bāb*, meaning "Gate" or "Category").

Instead of hundreds of random ways to add letters, there are only **10 major patterns** used in the modern and Quranic language. But on these 10 patterns thousand of verbs come. These are classified based on **how many extra letters** are added to the original 3-letter root.

---

The Classification of Mazīd Fīh  
The expanded verbs are grouped into three classes based on the "weight" of the extra letters added to the root.

Class 1: One Extra Letter (3 categories)

These are the most common. You add exactly one letter to the 3-letter root.

* **Bāb If'āl (إِفْعَال):** Adding an **Alif (Hamza)** at the beginning.  
  * *Effect:* Usually makes the verb transitive (making it happen to others).  
  * *Example:* **أَسْلَمَ** (He submitted/entered Islam) from $S-L-M$.  
* **Bāb Taf'īl (تَفْعِيل):** Adding a **Shadda** on the middle letter.  
  * *Effect:* Adds intensity or "teaching"/passing the action.  
  * *Example:* **عَلَّمَ** (He taught) from $'-L-M$.  
* **Bāb Mufā'alah (مُفَاعَلَة):** Adding an **Alif** after the first letter.  
  * *Effect:* Shows partnership or interaction between two people.  
  * *Example:* **قَاتَلَ** (He fought with someone) from $Q-T-L$.

Class 2: Two Extra Letters (5 categories)

These add more complexity, often shifting the meaning to the doer’s internal state or a mutual action.

* **Bāb Tafa''ul (تَفَعُّل):** Adding **Ta** and a **Shadda**.  
  * *Meaning:* Putting in effort or "becoming" something. (**تَعَلَّمَ** \- He learned).  
* **Bāb Tafā'ul (تَفَاعُل):** Adding **Ta** and an **Alif**.  
  * *Meaning:* Mutual action between a group. (**تَعَاوَنَ** \- They cooperated).  
* **Bāb Ifti'āl (اِفْتِعَال):** Adding an **Alif** at the start and a **Ta** after the first root letter.  
  * *Meaning:* To take something for oneself. (**اِجْتَهَدَ** \- He struggled/strived).  
* **Bāb Infi'āl (اِنْفِعَال):** Adding **Alif** and **Noon** at the start.  
  * *Meaning:* Passive/Reflexive (the thing happened). (**اِنْكَسَرَ** \- It broke).  
* **Bāb If'illāl (اِفْعِلَال):** Adding **Alif** and a **Shadda** on the last letter.  
  * *Meaning:* Usually for colors or physical defects. (**اِحْمَرَّ** \- He turned red).

Class 3: Three Extra Letters (2 main categories)

The "Heavyweights." These add three letters to the root.

* **Bāb Istif'āl (اِسْتِفْعَال):** Adding **Alif, Seen, and Ta** (**اِسْتَـ**).  
  * *Meaning:* To seek or request. (**اِسْتَغْفَرَ** \- He sought forgiveness).  
* **Bāb If'ī'āl (اِفْعِيعَال):** (Rare) Adding letters to show extreme intensity.

| Class | Pattern (Masdar) | Added Letters | Core Meaning |

I. The 6 Primary Doors (الثُّلَاثِيُّ الْمُجَرَّدُ)

**1\. بَاب فَتَحَ \- يَفْتَحُ** فَتَحَ، يَفْتَحُ، فَتْحاً، فَهُوَ فَاتِحٌ، وُفُتِحَ، يُفْتَحُ، فَهُوَ مَفْتُوحٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِفْتَحْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَفْتَحْ

**2\. بَاب نَصَرَ \- يَنْصُرُ** نَصَرَ، يَنْصُرُ، نَصْراً، فَهُوَ نَاصِرٌ، وَنُصِرَ، يُنْصَرُ، فَهُوَ مَنْصُورٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اُنْصُرْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَنْصُرْ

**3\. بَاب ضَرَبَ \- يَضْرِبُ** ضَرَبَ، يَضْرِبُ، ضَرْباً، فَهُوَ ضَارِبٌ، وَضُرِبَ، يُضْرَبُ، فَهُوَ مَضْرُوبٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِضْرِبْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَضْرِبْ

**4\. بَاب سَمِعَ \- يَسْمَعُ** سَمِعَ، يَسْمَعُ، سَمْعاً، فَهُوَ سَامِعٌ، وَسُمِعَ، يُسْمَعُ، فَهُوَ مَسْمُوعٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِسْمَعْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَسْمَعْ

**5\. بَاب حَسِبَ \- يَحْسِبُ** حَسِبَ، يَحْسِبُ، حِسْبَاناً، فَهُوَ حَاسِبٌ، وَحُسِبَ، يُحْسَبُ، فَهُوَ مَحْسُوبٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِحْسِبْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَحْسِبْ

**6\. بَاب كَرُمَ \- يَكْرُمُ** كَرُمَ، يَكْرُمُ، كَرَامَةً، فَهُوَ كَرِيمٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اُكْرُمْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَكْرُمْ

II. The 8 Increased Patterns (الثُّلَاثِيُّ الْمُزِيْدُ فِيْهِ)

**1\. بَاب إِفْعَال** أَفْعَلَ، يُفْعِلُ، إِفْعَالاً، فَهُوَ مُفْعِلٌ، وَأُفْعِلَ، يُفْعَلُ، فَهُوَ مُفْعَلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ أَفْعِلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تُفْعِلْ

**2\. بَاب تَفْعِيل** فَعَّلَ، يُفَعِّلُ، تَفْعِيلاً، فَهُوَ مُفَعِّلٌ، وَفُعِّلَ، يُفَعَّلُ، فَهُوَ مُفَعَّلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ فَعِّلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تُفَعِّلْ

**3\. بَاب مُفَاعَلَة** فَاعَلَ، يُفَاعِلُ، مُفَاعَلَةً، فَهُوَ مُفَاعِلٌ، وَفُوعِلَ، يُفَاعَلُ، فَهُوَ مُفَاعَلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ فَاعِلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تُفَاعِلْ

**4\. بَاب تَفَعُّل** تَفَعَّلَ، يَتَفَعَّلُ، تَفَعُّلاً، فَهُوَ مُتَفَعِّلٌ، وَتُفُعِّلَ، يُتَفَعَّلُ، فَهُوَ مُتَفَعَّلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ تَفَعَّلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَتَفَعَّلْ

**5\. بَاب تَفَاعُل** تَفَاعَلَ، يَتَفَاعَلُ، تَفَاعُلاً، فَهُوَ مُتَفَاعِلٌ، وَتُفُوعِلَ، يُتَفَاعَلُ، فَهُوَ مُتَفَاعَلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ تَفَاعَلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَتَفَاعَلْ

**6\. بَاب اِفْتِعَال** اِفْتَعَلَ، يَفْتَعِلُ، اِفْتِعَالاً، فَهُوَ مُفْتَعِلٌ، وَاُفْتُعِلَ، يُفْتَعَلُ، فَهُوَ مُفْتَعَلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِفْتَعِلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَفْتَعِلْ

**7\. بَاب اِنْفِعَال** اِنْفَعَلَ، يَنْفَعِلُ، اِنْفِعَالاً، فَهُوَ مُنْفَعِلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِنْفَعِلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَنْفَعِلْ

**8\. بَاب اِسْتِفْعَال** اِسْتَفْعَلَ، يَسْتَفْعِلُ، اِسْتِفْعَالاً، فَهُوَ مُسْتَفْعِلٌ، وَاُسْتُفْعِلَ، يُسْتَفْعَلُ، فَهُوَ مُسْتَفْعَلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِسْتَفْعِلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَسْتَفْعِلْ

#### Lesson 8.2 Quadrilitera Masdars  {#lesson-8.2-quadrilitera-masdars}

Think of the Arabic language as a massive construction site where almost every building is a **"Triangle"** (The 3-Letter Root). These triangles—like **K-T-B** (Writing) or **S-L-M** (Peace)—are the standard bricks used for 99% of the city.9

But, if you walk into the specialized districts of the language, you’ll find the **"Squares"** (The 4-Letter Root). These are the **Ruba'i** roots. They aren't just triangles with an extra piece glued on; they are built from the ground up with four unique corners that are inseparable.

1\. Ruba'i Mujarrad: بَاب فَعْلَلَة

This pattern is used for actions that are repetitive or involve four distinct root letters.

* **Example: بَعْثَرَ (To scatter/overturn — used in Surah Al-Infitar)** بَعْثَرَ، يُبَعْثِرُ، بَعْثَرَةً، فَهُوَ مُبَعْثِرٌ، وَبُعْثِرَ، يُبَعْثَرُ، فَهُوَ مُبَعْثَرٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ بَعْثِرْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تُبَعْثِرْ  
* **Example: وَسْوَسَ (To whisper — used in Surah An-Nas)** وَسْوَسَ، يُوَسْوِسُ، وَسْوَسَةً/وِسْوَاساً، فَهُوَ مُوَسْوِسٌ، وَوُسْوِسَ، يُوَسْوَسُ، فَهُوَ مُوَسْوَسٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ وَسْوِسْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تُوَسْوِسْ  
* **Example: زَلْزَلَ (To shake violently — used in Surah Az-Zalzalah)** زَلْزَلَ، يُزَلْزِلُ، زَلْزَلَةً/زِلْزَالاً، فَهُوَ مُزَلْزِلٌ، وَزُلْزِلَ، يُزَلْزَلُ، فَهُوَ مُزَلْزَلٌ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ زَلْزِلْ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تُزَلْزِلْ

2\. Ruba'i Mazid Fih: بَاب تَفَعْلُل

* This pattern often describes the result of an action or something happening to itself (reflexive).  
* **Example: تَرَدَّى (To fall headlong — used in Surah Al-Ma'idah regarding animals)** *Note: This is technically a 3-letter root doubled to 4, but follows this weight.* تَرَدَّى، يَتَرَدَّى، تَرَدِّياً، فَهُوَ مُتَرَدٍِّ، وَتُرُدِّيَ، يُتَرَدَّى، فَهُوَ مُتَرَدًّى، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ تَرَدَّ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَتَرَدَّ

3\. Ruba'i Mazid Fih: بَاب اِفْعِلَّال

* This pattern is used for deep internal states and is most famous for the word "Tranquility."  
* **Example: اِطْمَأَنَّ (To be at rest/peace — used in Surah Al-Fajr)** اِطْمَأَنَّ، يَطْمَأِنُّ، اِطْمِئْنَانًا، فَهُوَ مُطْمَئِنٌّ، وَاُطْمُئِنَّ، يُطْمَأَنُّ، فَهُوَ مُطْمَأَنٌّ، اَلْأَمْرُ مِنْهُ اِطْمَأِنَّ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنْهُ لَا تَطْمَأِنَّ

   ### **Module 9: The Nominative State (المَرْفُوعَاتُ)** {#module-9:-the-nominative-state-(المَرْفُوعَاتُ)}

Welcome to **Module 9**\! We have reached a major milestone. In Arabic grammar, there is a "VIP Club" of words called **المَرْفُوعَاتُ** (The Nominatives).

These are the words that **must** end in a **Damma** (or its equivalent state of *Raf'*). If you know who belongs in this club, you will rarely make a mistake in reading or speaking Arabic.

---

The "VIP Club" Members

There are **7 main types** of words that are always in the state of **رَفْعٌ**. We have already studied most of them\! Think of this module as the "Grand Review and Final Polish" of the Nominative state.

1\. The Subject of a Nominal Sentence (المُبْتَدَأُ)

* The word that starts a sentence.  
* *Example:* **زَيْدٌ عَالِمٌ** (Zayd is a scholar).

2\. The Predicate of a Nominal Sentence (الخَبَرُ)

* The news about the subject.  
* *Example:* **زَيْدٌ عَالِمٌ**.

3\. The Doer of a Verbal Sentence (الفَاعِلُ)

* The one who performed the action.  
* *Example:* **جَاءَ الحَقُّ** (The Truth has come)

.

4\. The Deputy Subject (نَائِبُ الفَاعِلِ)

* The object that took the doer's place in a Passive sentence (Lesson 8.3).  
* *Example:* **خُلِقَ الإِنْسَانُ** (Mankind was created).

5\. The Subject of Kāna (اِسْمُ كَانَ)

* Remember, Kāna leaves the first part alone (Lesson 7.1).  
* *Example:* **كَانَ اللهُ غَفُورًا**.

6\. The Predicate of Inna (خَبَرُ إِنَّ)

* Inna attacks the first part but leaves the second part alone (Lesson 7.2).  
* *Example:* **إِنَّ اللهَ غَفُورٌ**.

  ### **Module 10: The Accusative State (المَنْصُوبَاتُ)** {#module-10:-the-accusative-state-(المَنْصُوبَاتُ)}

#### **Lesson 10.1:** Direct Object (المَفْعُولُ بِهِ) {#lesson-10.1:-direct-object-(المَفْعُولُ-بِهِ)}

If I say to you: "The lion ate..." and then I stop talking. Did you understand the full story? Of course not\! You will immediately ask me: "What did the lion eat?".   
Look closely at the underlined words and notice the vowel (the Fat-ha) at the end of each one:

* رَكِبَ الفَارِسُ الجَوَادَ. (The knight rode the horse.)  
* قَرَأَ الطَّالِبُ القُرْآنَ. (The student read the Quran.)  
* تَبْنِي النَّحْلَةُ الخَلِيَّةَ. (The bee builds the hive.)  
* خَلَقَ اللهُ الأَرْضَ. (Allah created the Earth.)

In English grammar, we call this the Direct Object.

* The boy kicked the ball. (ball is the receiver of the kick).  
* The engineer built the bridge. (bridge is the thing being built).

The beauty of Arabic is that it provides a clear physical "stamp"—the Fat-ha—to tell you exactly which word is receiving the action.  
4\. Explanation  
The المَفْعُولُ بِهِ is the "target" in the sentence. It is the person or thing that the Action of the Doer fell upon.  
Think of a verbal sentence like an arrow being shot:

1. The Verb (فِعْلٌ) is the act of shooting.  
2. The Doer (فَاعِلٌ) is the person holding the bow.  
3. The Direct Object (مَفْعُولٌ بِهِ) is the target the arrow hits.

5\. Rules

1. Grammatical State: The المَفْعُولُ بِهِ is always in the state of نَصْبٌ (Accusative).  
2. The Sign: The primary sign for this state in a singular noun is the Fat-ha (ـَ).  
3. Position: It usually appears after the Doer (فَاعِلٌ) in a verbal sentence 

#### **Lesson 10.2:** Absolute Object (المَفْعُولُ المُطْلَقُ) {#lesson-10.2:-absolute-object-(المَفْعُولُ-المُطْلَقُ)}

Have you ever wanted to say something with **extra power**? Instead of just saying "I ran," you want to say "I ran a **serious** run\!" or "I hit the ball a **mighty** hit\!"

How does Arabic let us "turn up the volume" on a verb using its own name? That is the secret of the **Absolute Object**\!

**2\. Sentence Examples**

Notice how the underlined words look exactly like the **Verbs** at the start of the sentence. They share the same "DNA" (Root letters):

* **كَلَّمَ اللهُ مُوسَى تَكْلِيمًا.** (Allah spoke to Moses a \[direct\] **speaking**.)  
* **وَرَتِّلِ القُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا.** (And recite the Quran a \[proper\] **recitation**.)  
* **فَرِحَ النَّاجِحُ فَرَحًا شَدِيدًا.** (The successful person rejoiced a great **rejoicing**.)  
* **دَقَّتِ السَّاعَةُ دَقَّتَيْنِ.** (The clock struck two **strikings/chimes**.)

 In English we usually use **adverbs** or **extra phrases** to get the point across:

* *He slept **deeply**.* (In Arabic: He slept a **sleeping**.)  
* *She smiled **twice**.* (In Arabic: She smiled two **smilings**.)  
* *I truly **believe**.* (In Arabic: I believe a **believing**.)

The **المَفْعُولُ المُطْلَقُ** is a noun (specifically a **Masdar/ Infinitive**) derived from the same root as the verb in the sentence. It is called "Absolute" because it isn't tied to a specific person or time; it is the **pure essence** of the action.

We use it for three main reasons:

1. **To Emphasize:** To make sure the listener knows you *really* did it. (**قَفَزَ قَفْزًا** \- He *really* jumped).  
2. **To Describe the Style:** To show *how* it was done. (**نَامَ نَوْمَ الطِّفْلِ** \- He slept the *sleep of a child*).  
3. **To Count:** To show *how many times* it happened. (**سَجَدَ سَجْدَةً** \- He prostrated *one prostration*).

---

5\. Rules

1. **The "Twin" Rule:** The **المَفْعُولُ المُطْلَقُ** must share the same root letters as the verb in the sentence.  
2. **Grammatical State:** Like all members of the **مَفْعُولٌ** family, it is always **مَنْصُوبٌ** (Accusative).  
3. **The Sign:** Its primary sign is the **فَتْحَةٌ** (ـً).  
4. **Placement:** It always comes after the verb has been mentioned.

#### **Lesson 10.3:** Object of Purpose (المَفْعُولُ لِأَجْلِهِ) {#lesson-10.3:-object-of-purpose-(المَفْعُولُ-لِأَجْلِهِ)}

Have you ever done something specifically because of a feeling in your heart? You stood up **out of respect**, or you studied hard **hoping for success**.

In Arabic, how do we explain the "Heart's Reason" behind our actions in just one word? That is the role of the **Object of Purpose**\!

If the **Maf'ul Bihi** is the "Who/What" and the **Maf'ul Mutlaq** is the "Volume Knob," then the **Maf'ul Li-ajlihi (المَفْعُولُ لِأَجْلِهِ)** is the **"Why."**

It is the grammar of **intent** and **motivation**.

2\. Sentence Examples

Look at the underlined words. They answer the question: **"Why did you do it?"**

* **قَامَ الطَّالِبُ اِحْتِرَامًا لِلْمُعَلِّمِ.** (The student stood up **out of respect** for the teacher.)  
* **يُصَلِّي المُؤْمِنُ طَمَعًا فِي الجَنَّةِ.** (The believer prays **hoping** for Paradise.)  
* **تَصَدَّقْتُ ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاةِ اللهِ.** (I gave charity **seeking** the pleasure of Allah.)  
* **سَافَرْتُ طَلَبًا لِلْعِلْمِ.** (I traveled **in search** of knowledge.)

In English, we usually need a whole phrase starting with "because of," "out of," or "for the sake of":

* *He cried **out of fear**.*   
* *I stayed home **to rest**.*   
* *She helped him **out of kindness**.* 

5\. Rules

1. **The "Why" Rule:** The **المَفْعُولُ لِأَجْلِهِ** answers the question: **لِمَاذَا؟** (Why?).  
2. **Grammatical State:** As a member of the **مَفْعُولٌ** family, it is always **مَنْصُوبٌ** (Accusative).  
3. **The Sign:** Its primary sign is the **فَتْحَةٌ** (ـً).

#### **Lesson 10.4:** Adverb of Time/Place (المَفْعُولُ فِيهِ / الظَّرْف) {#lesson-10.4:-adverb-of-time/place-(المَفْعُولُ-فِيهِ-/-الظَّرْف)}

Every story needs a **Setting**. If I tell you "I met my friend," your very next questions will be "**When?**" and "**Where?**"

In Arabic, we have a special group of words that act like a "container" for the action, holding the time and the place inside them. This is the **Object of Time and Place**\!

Notice how the underlined words provide the "where" and "when" for the action, and look at the **Fat-ha** on their last letters:

**Adverbs of Time (ظَرْفُ زَمَانٍ):**

* **سَافَرْتُ لَيْلًا.** (I traveled **at night**.)  
* **صُمْتُ يَوْمَ الخَمِيسِ.** (I fasted on the **day** of Thursday.)  
* **يَهْطِلُ المَطَرُ شِتَاءً.** (The rain falls **in winter**.)

**Adverbs of Place (ظَرْفُ مَكَانٍ):**

* **جَلَسْتُ فَوْقَ الكُرْسِيِّ.** (I sat **above/on** the chair.)  
* **وَقَفَ الإِمَامُ أَمَامَ المُصَلِّينَ.** (The Imam stood **in front of** the worshippers.)  
* **بَنَى العُصْفُورُ عُشَّهُ بَيْنَ الأَغْصَانِ.** (The bird built its nest **between** the branches.)

In English, these are called **Adverbs** or **Prepositions of Time and Place**:

* *I will see you **tomorrow**.* (**tomorrow** is the time).  
* *The cat is **under** the table.* (**under** is the place).  
* *He waited for an **hour**.* (**hour** is the duration).

The **المَفْعُولُ فِيهِ** (also known as **الظَّرْف**) literally means "The Object *in* which" the action happens.

* Think of it as a **Box**: The action is placed inside this box of Time or Space.  
* **Time Adverbs (ظَرْفُ زَمَانٍ):** Answer the question **مَتَى؟** (When?).  
* **Place Adverbs (ظَرْفُ مَكَانٍ):** Answer the question **أَيْنَ؟** (Where?).

5\. Rules

1. **Grammatical State:** It is always **مَنْصُوبٌ** (Accusative).  
2. **The Sign:** Its primary sign is the **فَتْحَةٌ** (ـَ).  
3. **The Mudaf Rule:** Many adverbs of place (like **فَوْقَ**, **تَحْتَ**, **أَمَامَ**) act as a **مُضَافٌ**, meaning the word coming after them will have a **Kasra** (e.g., **فَوْقَ المَكْتَبِ**).

#### **Lesson 10.5:** Object of Accompaniment (المَفْعُولُ مَعَهُ) {#lesson-10.5:-object-of-accompaniment-(المَفْعُولُ-مَعَهُ)}

Have you ever walked along the beach while the sun was setting? You weren't exactly "walking the sun" like a dog, but you were walking alongside it.  
Or   
"Have you ever driven down a long highway at night while the moon followed you through the trees? You weren't exactly 'driving the moon' to its destination, but the moon was your silent passenger, keeping pace with every mile you covered."  
In Arabic, how do we describe an action that happens at the same time as something else, even if that thing isn't actually doing the action with us? That is the secret of the Object of Accompaniment\!  
2\. Sentence Examples  
Notice the special و (called the Waw of Ma'iyyah) and the Fat-ha on the underlined word that follows it:

* سَارَ الرَّجُلُ وَالجَبَلَ. (The man walked alongside/with the mountain.)  
* اِسْتَيْقَظْتُ وَطُلُوعَ الشَّمْسِ. (I woke up with the rising of the sun.)  
* مَشَى الطِّفْلُ وَالنَّهْرَ. (The child walked along the river.)  
* سَافَرَ المُسَافِرُ وَاللَّيْلَ. (The traveler traveled along with the night.)

In English, we usually use words like "along with," "at the same time as," or "alongside":

* I arrived with the sunset. (The sunset didn't drive me; it just happened as I arrived).  
* He walked along the wall.  
* We woke up with the dawn.

4\. Explanation  
The المَفْعُولُ مَعَهُ is a noun that comes after a specific و which means مَعَ (with/alongside).  
How is it different from a normal "And"?

* Normal "And" (حَرْفُ عَطْفٍ): Zayd and Amr ate. (Both are eating).  
* Waw of Accompaniment (وَاوُ المَعِيَّةِ): Zayd walked and the wall. (Zayd is walking; the wall is just there next to him).

The mountain, the river, or the sunrise are not "doing" the verb. They are simply the companions or the background to the action. Because they are "accompanying" the verb, they take a Fat-ha.  
5\. Rules

1. The "With" Rule: The و before the noun must mean "alongside" or "at the same time as."  
2. Grammatical State: Like the rest of its family, it is always مَنْصُوبٌ (Accusative).  
3. The Sign: Its primary sign is the فَتْحَةٌ (ـَ).  
4. The Doer Check: The noun after the و should be something that cannot logically share in doing the action (e.g., a man can walk, but a mountain cannot walk with him).

#### **Lesson 10.6:** Condition (الحَالُ) {#lesson-10.6:-condition-(الحَالُ)}

##### A {#a}

"Have you ever seen a child tell a lie while they were **shaking** with nerves? You aren't calling them a 'shaky child' in general, but you are describing the specific **vibe** they were radiating at the exact moment the words left their mouth." 

or

If I tell you, "The guest arrived," you know *who* came. But don't you want to know **how** he came? Did he arrive **smiling**? Was he **tired**? Was he **rushing**?

In Arabic, how do we "paint a picture" of the person's state at the exact moment the action happens? That is the power of the **Condition**\!

Look at the underlined words. They describe the **state** of the person while they are doing the verb:

* **جَاءَ الطِّفْلُ ضَاحِكًا.** (The child came **laughing**.)  
* **صَلَّى المُؤْمِنُ خَاشِعًا.** (The believer prayed **humbly**.)  
* **جَاءَ الطَّالِبُ مَسْرُورًا** (The student came **happy**.)  
* **شَرِبْتُ الْمَاءَ بَارِدًا** (I drank the water **\[while it was\] cold**.)

In English, we often use **Adverbs** (ending in \-ly) or **Participles** (ending in \-ing) to show the condition:

* *He ran **quickly**.*  
* *She entered the room **crying**.*  
* *The sun rose **brightly**.*

In Arabic, the **الحَالُ** functions like a "snapshot" taken at the time of the event.

The **الحَالُ** is a noun that describes the **manner** or **state** of the Doer (**الفَاعِلُ**) or the Object (**المَفْعُولُ بِهِ**) at the time the verb occurs.

Think of it as answering the question: **كَيْفَ؟** (How?).

**The two parts of the "Picture":**

1. **صَاحِبُ الحَالِ** (The Owner of the Condition): The person we are describing.  
2. **الحَالُ** (The Condition): The description itself (usually has **Tanween Fat-ha** and no **الـ**).

---

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

1. **The "How" Rule:** The **الحَالُ** answers the question **كَيْفَ؟** (How?).  
2. **Grammatical State:** It is always **مَنْصُوبٌ** (Accusative).  
3. **The Sign:** Its primary sign is the **فَتْحَةٌ** (ـً).  
4. **Indefinite Rule:** The **الحَالُ** itself is almost always **نَكِرَةٌ** (without **الـ**)

##### B. {#b.}

Now look in this sentence

* **رَأَيْتُ الْأُمَّ تَبْتَسِمُ** (I saw the mother **smiling**.)  
* **دَخَلَ الرَّجُلُ يَقْرَأُ الْقُرْآنَ** (The man entered **reading the Qur'an**.)  
* **وَصَلَ الْمُسَافِرُ وَقَدْ غَابَتِ الشَّمْسُ (The traveler arrived \[in the state that\] the sun had already set.)**  
* **عَادَ الْجَيْشُ وَقَدِ انْتَصَرَ (The army returned having \[already\] been victorious.)**  
* **خَرَجْتُ مِنَ الْبَيْتِ وَالْمَطَرُ يَنْزِلُ (I left the house while the rain was falling.)**  
* **سَافَرْتُ وَأَنَا صَغِيرٌ (I traveled while I was \[still\] young.)**

#### **Lesson 10.7:** Distinction (التَّمْيِيزُ) {#lesson-10.7:-distinction-(التَّمْيِيزُ)}

"Have you ever had a friend say, 'I have twenty,' and then just stop talking? You know they have the number 20, but you're left wondering: Twenty what? Dollars? Cats? Problems? The **Tamyiz** is the word that drops in to **clear the fog** and specify exactly what that 'twenty' refers to." 

Or

**Tamyiz (التَّمْيِيزُ)** is the **"Magnifying Glass."** It steps in when a sentence is technically complete but leaves the listener confused or squinting at a vague idea. 

2\. أَمْثِلَةٌ مِنَ الجُمَلِ (Sentence Examples)

Notice how the underlined words remove the "mystery" from the word before them:

* **اِشْتَرَيْتُ عِشْرِينَ كِتَابًا.** (I bought twenty **books**.)  
* **طَابَ مُحَمَّدٌ نَفْسًا.** (Muhammad was happy **in soul/spirit**.)  
* **فَجَّرْنَا الأَرْضَ عُيُونًا.** (We caused the earth to gush forth with **springs**.)  
* **أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنْكَ مَالًا.** (I am more than you **in wealth**.)

In English, we often use the words **"in,"** **"of,"** or just the noun itself to clarify:

* *He is the best **in height**.*  
* *A kilo **of grapes**.*  
* *He is 15 **years** old.*

**التَّمْيِيزُ** is a noun used to remove ambiguity (vagueness) from a previous word or an entire sentence.

It generally falls into two categories:

1. **Distinction of a Single Word (ذات):** Clarifying numbers, weights, or measurements.  
   * *Example:* "I have a liter (**of what?**) **لَبَنًا** (milk)."  
2. **Distinction of a Sentence (نِسْبَة):** Clarifying a relationship.  
   * *Example:* "The student is the most hardworking (**in what?**) **أَدَبًا** (in manners)."

Without the **التَّمْيِيزُ**, the sentence would be a riddle\!

**5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)**

1. **The "Clarifier" Rule:** It answers the silent question: **"In what way?"** or **"Exactly what?"**.  
2. **Grammatical State:** It is always **مَنْصُوبٌ** (Accusative).  
3. **The Sign:** Its primary sign is the **فَتْحَةٌ** (ـً).  
4. **Indefinite Rule:** It must be **نَكِرَةٌ** (without **الـ**).  
5. **Standard Pattern:** You will very often see it after "Comparative" words (words like **أَكْثَرُ** \- more, **أَجْمَلُ** \- more beautiful, **أَكْبَرُ** \- bigger).

The relationship between **Al-'Adad** (The Number) and **Al-Ma'dūd** (The Numbered Item) is one of the most technical areas of Arabic grammar. It is often called the "Grammar of the Opposites" because the rules of gender frequently flip-flop.

Think of it as a **Dance of Agreement and Disagreement**.

---

1\. Numbers 1 and 2: The Perfect Match  
Numbers 1 and 2 act like regular adjectives. They follow the noun they describe in everything: Gender, Case, and Number.

* **Rule:** The noun comes first, the number comes second.  
* **Gender:** They match perfectly.  
* **Examples:**  
  * **كِتَابٌ وَاحِدٌ** (One book \- Both masculine)  
  * **بِنْتَانِ اثْنَتَانِ** (Two girls \- Both feminine)

---

2\. Numbers 3 to 10: The "Grumpy" Opposites  
This is the most famous rule. The number and the noun **hate** to be the same gender.

* **Gender Rule:** If the noun is masculine, the number must be feminine (with a *Ta Marbuta*). If the noun is feminine, the number must be masculine.  
* **The "Plural Rule":** The noun must be **Plural** and **Majrur** (ending in a "Kasra" or "in" sound).  
* **The Origin Rule:** You check the gender of the noun based on its **singular** form, not the plural.

| Number | Noun (Singular) | Correct Phrase | Why? |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **3** | **رَجُل** (Man \- Masc) | **ثَلَاثَةُ رِجَالٍ** | Number is fem, Noun is plural. |
| **5** | **سَيَّارَة** (Car \- Fem) | **خَمْسُ سَيَّارَاتٍ** | Number is masc, Noun is plural. |

---

3\. Numbers 11 and 12: The Harmonious Teens  
Numbers 11 and 12 return to being "friendly." Both parts of the number (the unit and the ten) match the noun.

* **Rule:** The noun must be **Singular** and **Mansub** (ending in "-an").  
* **Gender:** Full agreement.  
* **Examples:**  
  * **أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا** (Eleven stars \- All masculine)  
  * **اثْنَتَا عَشْرَةَ عَيْنًا** (Twelve springs \- All feminine)

---

4\. Numbers 13 to 19: The "Split Personality"  
In these compound numbers, the "Unit" (3-9) stays "grumpy" (opposite gender), but the "Ten" (10) becomes "friendly" (matches the noun).

* **Rule:** The noun must be **Singular** and **Mansub** (-an).  
* **Gender:** Unit (Opposite) \+ Ten (Matches) \+ Noun.  
* **Example:**  
  * **ثَلَاثَةَ عَشَرَ طَالِبًا** (13 Students \[M\]) — *3 is fem, 10 is masc, Student is masc.*  
  * **تِسْعَ عَشْرَةَ بِنْتًا** (19 Girls \[F\]) — *9 is masc, 10 is fem, Girl is fem.*

---

5\. Multiples of Ten (20, 30... 90): The "Uqud"  
These numbers are the easiest. They don't care about gender at all. They look the same whether you are counting lions or roses.

* **Rule:** The noun must be **Singular** and **Mansub** (-an).  
* **Grammar:** They follow the rules of "Sound Masculine Plural" (ending in *\-una* or *\-ina*).  
* **Example:**  
  * **عِشْرُونَ رَجُلًا** (20 men)  
  * **عِشْرُونَ امْرَأَةً** (20 women)

---

6\. Numbers 100, 1000, and Millions  
These are "High Society" numbers. They are formal and rigid.

* **Gender:** They stay exactly the same regardless of gender.  
* **Rule:** The noun must be **Singular** and **Majrur** (-in).  
* **Example:**  
  * **مِائَةُ كِتَابٍ** (100 books)  
  * **أَلْفُ لَيْلَةٍ** (1000 nights)

####  **Lesson 10.8: The Vocative (المُنَادَى \- Al-Munada)**. {#lesson-10.8:-the-vocative-(المُنَادَى---al-munada).}

"Have you ever been in a crowded room where everyone is talking, and suddenly someone shouts your name? In that split second, you are pulled out of the 'background' of the room and placed under a **grammatical spotlight**. You aren't just a person anymore; you are the **intended target** of a message." 

Or 

If you are in a crowded market and you want your friend’s attention, what is the first thing you do? You shout their name\! But did you know that in Arabic, the moment you call someone's name, the grammar of that name might change?

How does the little word "**يَا**" act like a "Change-Agent" for the person being called? Let's discover the world of the **Vocative**\!

Observe how the ending of the name changes depending on how we call the person:

* **يَا زَيْدُ، انْتَبِهْ.** (O **Zayd**, pay attention.)  
* **يَا عَبْدَ اللهِ، أَقْبِلْ.** (O **Abdullah**, come forward.)  
* **يَا طَالِبًا، اجْتَهِدْ.** (O **student** \[any student\], work hard.)  
* **يَا رَجُلُ، سَاعِدْنِي.** (O **man** \[a specific one in front of me\], help me.)

In English, we usually just say the name, or sometimes add "O" in formal or religious contexts:

* *Hey **Zayd**\!*  
* *O **God**, help us\!*  
* *O **Servant of Allah**.*

The **المُنَادَى** is the noun that comes after a tool used for calling (the most famous tool being **يَا**).

The **المُنَادَى** is actually considered part of the **مَنْصُوبَاتُ** (the Fat-ha family) because, in reality, when you say "O Zayd," you are saying "I am calling Zayd." Since "Zayd" is the object of your calling, he should be **مَنْصُوبٌ**.

However, the "VIPs" (specific names or people right in front of you) get a special honor—they take a **Damma** instead of a Fat-ha to show they are "Fixed" and "Known."

**The two main types you need to know:**

1. **The "Fixed" (المَبْنِيّ):** If it is a single name (**زَيْدُ**) or a specific person you are looking at (**رَجُلُ**), it ends with a **Damma**.  
2. **The "Accusative" (المَنْصُوب):** If the name is a phrase (**عَبْدَ اللهِ**) or you are calling someone general (**طَالِبًا**), it takes a **Fat-ha**.

---

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

1. **The Tool:** The most common word for calling is **يَا**.  
2. **Grammatical State:** The **المُنَادَى** is fundamentally **مَنْصُوبٌ** (Accusative).  
3. **The Damma Exception:** If the name is a **single word** (like a proper name), it takes a **Damma** (without Tanween).  
4. **The Fat-ha Rule:** If the name is **Mudaf** (a two-word possessive phrase), it **must** take a **Fat-ha**.  
5. **Calling "Allah":** When calling Allah, we say **يَا اللهُ** or we say **اللَّهُمَّ** (the "Meem" at the end replaces the "Ya" at the beginning).

#### **Lesson 10.8:** The Exception (الاِسْتِثْنَاءُ) {#lesson-10.8:-the-exception-(الاِسْتِثْنَاءُ)}

"Have you ever been at a party where everyone decided to leave, but one person stayed behind to help clean up? You wouldn't say 'everyone stayed,' and you wouldn't say 'everyone left.' You would say, 'Everyone left **except** Zaid.' The **Istithna** is the grammatical gatekeeper that stands at the exit and says, 'Everyone passes through... but not you.'" 

Or 

"Have you ever looked at a garden where every single flower was red, but there was one lone white daisy in the middle? Your brain processes the 'group' first, and then it focuses on the **exception**. The **Istithna** is that 'Wait a minute' moment in a sentence that highlights the one thing that doesn't fit the rule." 

In Arabic, "The Exception" is like a mathematical minus sign ($-$). It allows us to make a general statement and then "subtract" one specific part from it. Let's see how this subtraction changes the grammar\!

---

2\. أَمْثِلَةٌ مِنَ الجُمَلِ (Sentence Examples)  
Pay close attention to the word that comes after the "Exception Tool" **إِلَّا** (except):

* **حَضَرَ الضُّيُوفُ إِلَّا وَاحِدًا** (The guests arrived, except one.)  
* **نَجَحَ الطُّلَّابُ إِلَّا زَيْدًا** (The students passed, except Zayd.)  
* **أَكَلْتُ الطَّعَامَ إِلَّا خُبْزًا** (I ate the food, except bread.)

* **مَا حَضَرَ الضُّيُوفُ إِلَّا وَاحِدٌ** (No guests arrived, except one.)  
* **مَا نَجَحَ الطُّلَّابُ إِلَّا زَيْدٌ** (No students passed, except Zayd.)  
    
* **وَصَلَ الْمُسَافِرُونَ إِلَّا حَقَائِبَهُمْ** (The travelers arrived, except their luggage.)  
* **فَسَجَدَ الْمَلَائِكَةُ كُلُّهُمْ أَجْمَعُونَ إِلَّا إِبْلِيسَ** (The angels prostrated, all of them together, except Iblis.)

* **مَا مُحَمَّدٌ إِلَّا رَسُولٌ** (Muhammad is not but a messenger.)  
* **مَا قُلْتُ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ** (I did not say anything except the truth.)

In English, we use words like "except," "but," or "excluding":

* *Everyone went home **but** Sarah.*  
* *I like all fruits **except** apples.*  
* *All the lights were off **excluding** one.*

In Arabic, the word "except" (**إِلَّا**) acts like a gatekeeper. Once a noun passes through this gate, its vowel often switches to a **Fat-ha**.

---

4\. الشَّرْحُ (Explanation)  
**الاِسْتِثْنَاءُ** is the process of excluding a noun (the *Mustathna*) from the ruling that applies to the group before it (the *Mustathna Minhu*).

Think of it in three parts:

1. **المُسْتَثْنَى مِنْهُ** (The Big Group): The ones the action originally applied to.  
2. **أَدَاةُ الاِسْتِثْنَاءِ** (The Tool): The word "**إِلَّا**" (Except).  
3. **المُسْتَثْنَى** (The Exception): The specific one being subtracted.

#### **Lesson 12.2:** Emphasis (التَّوْكِيدُ) {#lesson-12.2:-emphasis-(التَّوْكِيدُ)}

Have you ever told someone a piece of news, and they looked at you with disbelief? You say, "The teacher came," and they ask, "Really? The teacher himself?"  
In Arabic, how do we remove doubt from the listener's mind and prove that we mean exactly what we said? We use The Emphasis\! It is the "Bodyguard" of a word that follows it everywhere to make sure its meaning is clear.  
2\. أَمْثِلَةٌ مِنَ الجُمَلِ (Sentence Examples)  
Notice how the underlined words repeat or reinforce the word before them. Look closely at the vowels:

* جَاءَ المَلِكُ نَفْسُهُ. (The King himself came.)  
* نَجَحَ الطُّلَّابُ كُلُّهُمْ. (The students, all of them, succeeded.)  
* رَأَيْتُ الوَزِيرَ عَيْنَهُ. (I saw the Minister himself.)  
* لَا لَا، لَنْ أَذْهَبَ. (No, no, I will not go.)

In English, we use "Reflexive Pronouns" or repetition for emphasis:

* I did it myself.  
* The President himself signed the law.  
* They all arrived on time.  
* Stop\! Stop\!

In Arabic, التَّوْكِيدُ is a "Follower" (تَابِعٌ). This means it mimics the grammar of the word it is emphasizing. If the leader has a Damma, the bodyguard gets a Damma\!  
There are two main ways to emphasize:

1. Verbal Emphasis (التَّوْكِيدُ اللَّفْظِيُّ): Simply repeating the word (Noun, Verb, or Particle).  
   * Example: جَاءَ جَاءَ الحَقُّ (The Truth has come, has come).  
2. Moral/Meaningful Emphasis (التَّوْكِيدُ المَعْنَوِيُّ): Using specific "Power Words" like:  
   * نَفْس / عَيْن (Self/Same) — To confirm the specific person.  
   * كُل / جَمِيع (All/Entirety) — To confirm no one was left out.

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

1. The Follower Rule: The Emphasis (التَّوْكِيد) follows the word it emphasizes (المُؤَكَّد) in its ending vowel (Damma, Fat-ha, or Kasra).  
2. The Pronoun Link: When using words like نَفْس or كُل, you must attach a pronoun that points back to the leader (e.g., نَفْسُـهُ, كُلُّـهُمْ).  
3. Removal Test: If you remove the emphasis word, the sentence should still make basic sense.  
   * Example: جَاءَ المَلِكُ (The King came) still works without نَفْسُهُ.

#### **Lesson 12.3:** Substitution (البَدَلُ) {#lesson-12.3:-substitution-(البَدَلُ)}

Have you ever introduced someone by their title and then their name? For example: "The leader, **Zayd**, arrived."

If I removed the word "leader," the sentence still works: "Zayd arrived." If I removed "Zayd," it also works: "The leader arrived." In Arabic, when one word can completely "replace" or "substitute" for another while keeping the same meaning, we call it **The Substitution**.

How does this "Word-Twin" follow the grammar of its partner?

---

2\. أَمْثِلَةٌ مِنَ الجُمَلِ (Sentence Examples)

Notice how the underlined words are the "Substitutes" for the words before them. They share the **exact same vowel**:

* **جَاءَ الخَلِيفَةُ عُمَرُ.** (The Caliph, **Umar**, came.)  
* **نَفَعَنِي المُرَبِّي نُصْحُهُ.** (The mentor benefited me, \[specifically\] **his advice**.)  
* **أَكَلْتُ الرَّغِيفَ ثُلُثَهُ.** (I ate the loaf, **a third of it**.)  
* **أُحِبُّ هَذَا العَالِمَ.** (I love this **scholar**.)

In English, this is often called **Apposition**:

* *My brother **Abdullah** is traveling.* (**Abdullah** is a substitute for "my brother").  
* *I visited the capital, **Cairo**.* (**Cairo** is a substitute for "the capital").  
* *I read the book, its **introduction**.* (Specifically substituting the whole book for a part of it).

In Arabic, the **بَدَلٌ** is the ultimate "Follower." It mimics the case (Damma, Fat-ha, or Kasra) of the word it is substituting for.

There are three main types:

1. **Total Substitution (بَدَلُ كُلٍّ مِنْ كُلٍّ):** The substitute is the *exact same* thing as the first word.  
   * *Example:* **الفَارُوقُ عُمَرُ** (The Farooq \[is\] Umar).  
2. **Partial Substitution (بَدَلُ بَعْضٍ مِنْ كُلٍّ):** The substitute is a *physical part* of the first word.  
   * *Example:* **سَقَطَ البَيْتُ سَقْفُهُ** (The house fell, \[specifically\] its roof).  
3. **Qualitative Substitution (بَدَلُ اشْتِمَالٍ):** The substitute is a *quality or meaning* related to the first word (not a physical part).  
   * *Example:* **أَعْجَبَنِي الزَّهْرُ أَرِيجُهُ** (The flower amazed me, \[specifically\] its scent).

### **Module 13: Advanced Syntax (أَسَالِيبُ الجُمْلَةِ)** {#module-13:-advanced-syntax-(أَسَالِيبُ-الجُمْلَةِ)}

#### **Lesson 13.1:** Conditional Sentences (أُسْلُوبُ الشَّرْطِ) {#lesson-13.1:-conditional-sentences-(أُسْلُوبُ-الشَّرْطِ)}

Welcome to the grammar of **"The Deal."**

The **Conditional Sentence (Ash-Shart)** is a contract between two actions. It says: "If Action A happens, then Action B will definitely follow." In Arabic, the "flavor" of the deal changes depending on which word you use to start it.

"Have you ever looked at a light switch? It’s a simple condition: **If** you flip the switch, **then** the light turns on. The Arabic conditional tools are like different types of switches—some are for things that will definitely happen, some are for 'maybe' situations, and some are for 'impossible' dreams that never came true."

The Three Flavors of "If"

A.  (إِنْ) — The "Pure Maybe"

"Have you ever made a bet with a friend? 'If you study, you'll pass.' You don't know for sure if they will study, but you're setting the rule. **In** is the most basic, logical tool. It's used for possibilities that haven't happened yet."

* **The Vibe:** A neutral, future possibility.  
* **The Grammar:** It often makes both verbs "chopped" (**Majzum**) or shortened.

B.  (إِذَا) — The "When/Certainty"

"Have you ever waited for the sun to rise? You don't say 'If the sun rises' as if it might stay in bed. You say 'If (When) the sun rises.' **Idhā** is used for things that are expected or guaranteed to happen. It feels more like 'when' than 'if'."

* **The Vibe:** High certainty or frequent occurrence.  
* **The Grammar:** It usually precedes a past-tense verb, even if we are talking about the future.

C.  (لَوْ) — The "Impossible Dream"

"Have you ever sighed and said, 'If I were a bird, I would fly away'? You aren't a bird, and you aren't flying. **Law** is the 'Veto' of the past. It’s used for things that **did not happen**, so the result **could not happen**. It’s the grammar of regret or imagination."

* **The Vibe:** Hypotheses or impossibilities.  
* **The Grammar:** The second part often starts with a **La (لَ)** for emphasis.

Eg:

* **إِنْ تَجْتَهِدْ تَنْجَحْ** (If you study, you will succeed.) — *\[Possible\]*  
* **إِذَا جَاءَ نَصْرُ اللَّهِ وَالْفَتْحُ** (When the victory of Allah comes...) — *\[Certain\]*  
* **لَوْ كُنْتُ غَنِيًّا لَتَصَدَّقْتُ** (If I were rich \[but I'm not\], I would have given charity.) — *\[Impossible/Hypothetical\]*

##### A {#a-1}

When is Fā (فَـ) added?  
The **Fā** is called *Fā al-Jazā’* (The Fā of Recompense). Think of it as a **"Grammatical Bridge."** You only need it when the second half of the sentence (the result) is too "heavy" or "complex" to be a simple verb.

You must add **Fā** if the result is:

* **A Noun Sentence:** *If you study, **then** success is yours.* (**إِنْ تَجْتَهِدْ فَـالنَّجَاحُ حَلِيفُكَ**)  
* **A Command:** *If you see him, **then** tell him.* (**إِنْ رَأَيْتَهُ فَـقُلْ لَهُ**)  
* **Starts with specific words:** Like **قَدْ** (already), **سَـ / سَوْفَ** (future), or **مَا** (negation).  
  * *Example:* *If you travel, **then** I will surely miss you.* (**إِنْ تُسَافِرْ فَـسَوْفَ أَشْتَاقُ إِلَيْكَ**)

**The Rule:** If the result cannot be "chopped" with a Sukun (because it's a noun or a command), the **Fā** must step in to protect it.

##### B {#b}

 When is Lām (لَـ) added?  
The Lām is the "Emphasizer." Its job is to scream, "This definitely would have happened\!"

* Used with: لَوْ (Law)  
* The Job: It almost exclusively attaches to the result of an "Impossible/Hypothetical" sentence.  
* The Vibe: It adds a layer of "would have" or "certainly" to the imaginary outcome.  
* Example: If you had invited me, I certainly would have come. (لَوْ دَعَوْتَنِي لَـحَضَرْتُ)


##### C {#c}

Words Similar to In (إِنْ)  
These are called the **Jazim** tools. They behave exactly like *In* (they add a Sukun to two verbs), but they add a specific "flavor" of meaning:

| Word | Meaning | Example |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **مَنْ (Man)** | **Whoever** | *Whoever does good, will see it.* (**مَنْ يَعْمَلْ خَيْرًا يَرَهُ**) |
| **مَا (Mā)** | **Whatever** | *Whatever you spend, Allah knows it.* (**مَا تُنْفِقُوا يَعْلَمْهُ اللهُ**) |
| **مَتَى (Matā)** | **Whenever** | *Whenever you go, I go.* (**مَتَى تَذْهَبْ أَذْهَبْ**) |
| **أَيْنَ (Ayna)** | **Wherever** | *Wherever you are, death will find you.* (**أَيْنَمَا تَكُونُوا يُدْرِكْكُمُ الْمَوْتُ**) |

#### 13.2 The Imperative and its Result: (الطَّلَبُ وَجَوابُ الطَّلَبِ) {#13.2-the-imperative-and-its-result:-(الطَّلَبُ-وَجَوابُ-الطَّلَبِ)}

"Think of a vending machine. You don't have to say, 'If I press this button, then the soda will fall.' The machine is designed so that the **action** itself triggers the **result**. **Talab** is you pressing the button (the command), and the **Jawab** is the soda falling out (the result). 

Have you ever given someone a command and told them exactly what would happen if they followed it? For example: "Study hard—you will pass." Or, "Be kind—people will love you."

In Arabic, the relationship between a **Request** and its **Result** is very strong. If you start a sentence with a command (The Request), the verb that follows as a result gets a special "Grammatical Stamp." Let’s see how the **Request** and its **Result** work together\!

Notice the first word (the Command) and the last word (the Result). Look closely at the **Sukun** (ـْ) on the second verb:

* **اِجْتَهِدْ تَنْجَحْ.** (Study hard—\[and as a result\] **you will pass**.)  
* **أَنْفِقْ تُؤْجَرْ.** (Spend in charity—\[and as a result\] **you will be rewarded**.)  
* **اِبْتَسِمْ تَفْرَحْ.** (Smile—\[and as a result\] **you will be happy**.)  
* **لَا تَغْضَبْ تَدْخُلِ الجَنَّةَ.** (Do not get angry—\[and as a result\] **you will enter** Paradise.)

In English, we often use a dash or the word "and" to connect a command to its outcome:

* *Work out, **you’ll get fit**.*  
* *Help the poor **and you will find peace**.*  
* *Don't lie, **you'll be trusted**.*

It has two main pillars:

1. **الطَّلَبُ (The Request):** This is usually a Command (**أَمْر**) or a Prohibition (**نَهْي**).  
2. **جَوَابُ الطَّلَبِ (The Result):** This is the Present Tense verb (**مُضَارِع**) that happens because of the request.

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

1. **The Start:** The sentence must begin with a **Request** (Command, Prohibition, or even a Question).  
2. **The Result:** The result must be a **Present Tense verb** (**مُضَارِع**).  
3. **The Vowel:** The result verb takes a **Sukun** (or drops its final vowel if it's a "weak" letter).  
4. **The Logical Link:** The result must be something that logically follows the command.  
   * *Correct:* "Study, you will succeed."  
   * *Incorrect:* "Study, the sun will rise." (The sun rising isn't a result of your studying\!)  
5. **Prohibition Rule:** If the request is a "Don't" (**نَهْي**), the result must be something **praiseworthy**.  
   * *Example:* "Don't get close to the fire—you will be safe (**تَسْلَمْ**)."

#### **Lesson 14.3:** The Diminutive: (التَّصْغِيرُ) {#lesson-14.3:-the-diminutive:-(التَّصْغِيرُ)}

"Imagine you have a full-sized car. If you want to talk about a tiny toy version of it, you don't just call it a 'small car'; you give it a specific 'smallness' shape. In Arabic, **Tasghir** is like putting a word into a special mold that instantly shrinks it. 

How do you turn something big into something small, cute, or dear to your heart using just a few vowels? If you have a "Book" (**كِتَاب**) and you want to call it a "Little Book," or a "Son" (**اِبْن**) and you want to call him "My Dear Little Son," how do you do it in one word?

In Arabic, we have a "Melody" called **التَّصْغِيرُ**. It’s like a shrinking machine for nouns\!

Notice how the words change their shape to sound like the pattern **فُعَيْل**:

* **يَا بُنَيَّ، أَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ.** (O **my dear little son**, establish prayer.)  
* **قَرَأْتُ كُتَيِّبًا نَافِعًا.** (I read a **little book/booklet**.)  
* **نَلْتَقِي قُبَيْلَ المَغْرِبِ.** (We will meet **just shortly before** Maghrib.)  
* **هَذَا جُبَيْلٌ صَغِيرٌ.** (This is a **small mountain/hill**.)

In English, we usually add "little" or use special suffixes like "-let" or "-y":

* *Book* → *Book**let***  
* *River* → *Rivu**let***  
* *Dog* → *Dogg**ie***

**Why do we use it?**

1. **Smallness:** To show something is physically small (e.g., **كَلْب** → **كُلَيْب** "Small dog").  
2. **Endearment:** To show love (e.g., **أَخ** → **أُخَيّ** "Dear brother").  
3. **Shortness of Time/Distance:** (e.g., **بَعْد** → **بُعَيْد** "Just a little after").  
4. **Belittling:** To show something is unimportant or weak.

   1\. The 3-Letter Pattern: فُعَيْل (Fu’ayl)

This is used for nouns that consist of three original letters.

* **كَلْب (Kalb \- Dog) → كُلَيْب (Kulayb \- Little dog)**  
* **نَهْر (Nahr \- River) → نُهَيْر (Nuhayr \- Small river/Stream)**  
* **رَجُل (Rajul \- Man) → رُجَيْل (Rujayl \- Little man)**  
* **أَسَد (Asad \- Lion) → أُسَيْد (Usayd \- Little lion)**  
* **بَحْر (Bahr \- Sea) → بُحَيْر (Buhayr \- Small sea/Lake)**

  2\. The 4-Letter Pattern: فُعَيْعِل (Fu’ay’il)

This is used for nouns that consist of four original letters. You keep the first three sounds but add an "i" sound at the end.

* **دِرْهَم (Dirham \- Dirham) → دُرَيْهِم (Durayhim \- Little dirham)**  
* **مَنْزِل (Manzil \- House) → مُنَيْزِل (Munayzil \- Small house)**  
* **مَسْجِد (Masjid \- Mosque) → مُسَيْجِد (Musayjid \- Small mosque)**  
* **ثَعْلَب (Tha’lab \- Fox) → ثُعَيْلِب (Thu’aylib \- Little fox)**  
* **جَعْفَر (Ja’far \- Name) → جُعَيْفِر (Ju’ayfir \- Little Ja'far)**

  3\. The 5-Letter Pattern (with long vowel): فُعَيْعِيل (Fu’ay’īl)

This is used for five-letter nouns where the fourth letter is a "long vowel" (like Alif, Waw, or Ya). That vowel always turns into a long **Ya (ـيـ)**.

* **مِفْتَاح (Miftāh \- Key) → مُفَيْتِيح (Mufaytīh \- Tiny key)**  
* **عُصْفُور (Usfūr \- Bird) → عُصَيْفِير (Usayfīr \- Little bird/Chick)**  
* **مِصْبَاح (Misbāh \- Lamp) → مُصَيْبِيح (Musaybīh \- Small lamp)**  
* **قِنْدِيل (Qindīl \- Lantern) → قُنَيْدِيل (Qunaydīl \- Small lantern)**


  Special Case: Feminine Nouns without Ta Marbuta

If a three-letter noun is feminine but doesn't have a **ة** (like "Sun" or "Hand"), we add the **ة** back when we shrink it to show its true gender.

* **شَمْس (Shams \- Sun) → شُمَيْسَة (Shumaysah \- Little sun)**  
* **هِنْد (Hind \- Name/India) → هُنَيْدَة (Hunaydah \- Little Hind)**  
* **أُذُن (Udhun \- Ear) → أُذَيْنَة (Udhaynah \- Small ear)**

#### **14.4 Relative Nouns: (النِّسْبَةُ)** {#14.4-relative-nouns:-(النِّسْبَةُ)}

"Imagine you have a group of people standing in a field. They are all just individuals. But then, you give one person a **Blue Jersey**, another a **Red Jersey**, and another a **Green Jersey**. Suddenly, they aren't just 'people' anymore—they are 'The Blue Team,' 'The Red Team,' and 'The Green Team.' 

If you meet someone from **Egypt** (**مِصْر**), how do you describe them in one word? You say they are **Egyptian** (**مِصْرِيّ**).

In Arabic, how do we take a noun and turn it into an adjective that shows **origin**, **belonging**, or **nationality**? We use a "Super-Suffix" called the **Nisbah**. It’s like a flag you attach to a word to show where it comes from\!

Notice the ending of the underlined words—a heavy "**Ya**" with a Shaddah (ـِيّ):

* **أَنَا طَالِبٌ عَرَبِيٌّ.** (I am an **Arab** student.)  
* **هَذَا سِجَّادٌ تُرْكِيٌّ.** (This is a **Turkish** carpet.)  
* **القُرْآنُ كِتَابٌ سَمَاوِيٌّ.** (The Quran is a **heavenly** book.)

In English, we change the endings of words to show belonging, but the endings are always different and sometimes confusing:

* *America* → *America**n***  
* *Britain* → *Brit**ish***  
* *China* → *Chin**ese***  
* *History* → *Histori**cal***

In Arabic, it is much simpler\! Almost every word uses the same "Double-Ya" ending to achieve this.

**Why do we use it?**

* **Nationality:** (e.g., **كَنَدَا** → **كَنَدِيّ** "Canadian").  
* **Religion/School of Thought:** (e.g., **إِسْلَام** → **إِسْلَامِيّ** "Islamic").  
* **Nature/Quality:** (e.g., **ذَهَب** \[Gold\] → **ذَهَبِيّ** \[Golden\]).

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

1. **The Basic Rule:** Add **ـِيّ** to the end and put a **Kasra** on the letter before it.  
   * **مَكَّة** → **مَكِّيّ**.  
2. **The "Ta Marbuta" Rule:** If the word ends in **ة**, you **must** delete it before adding the Nisbah.  
   * **مَدِينَة** (City) → **مَدَنِيّ** (Civic/Urban).  
   * **فَلَسْطِين** → **فَلَسْطِينِيّ**.  
3. **Feminine Form:** If you are describing a female or a feminine noun, simply add a **Ta Marbuta** to the end of the Nisbah.  
   * **مِصْرِيّ** (Male Egyptian) → **مِصْرِيَّة** (Female Egyptian).

#### **Lesson 14.5:** Vocative/Calling: (النِّدَاءُ) {#lesson-14.5:-vocative/calling:-(النِّدَاءُ)}

#### **Lesson 14.6:** Lamentation: (النُّدْبَةُ) {#lesson-14.6:-lamentation:-(النُّدْبَةُ)}

Have you ever felt a pain so deep—either in your body or your heart—that a simple "ouch" wasn't enough? Or have you ever mourned a great loss and wanted to call out to the world?

In Arabic, there is a special, dramatic way to express **deep pain** or **lamentation**. It sounds like a long, echoing cry. This is called **النُّدْبَةُ**.

Notice the "Wa" at the beginning and the long "Ah" sound at the end of the underlined words:

* **وَارَأْسَاه\!** (O, my \[aching\] **head**\!)  
* **وَاقَلْبَاه\!** (O, my \[breaking\] **heart**\!)  
* **وَامُعْتَصِمَاه\!** (O, **Mu'tasim**\! — *The famous historical cry for help*)  
* **وَاحُزْنَاه\!** (O, my **sorrow**\!)

In English, we use "O," "Alas," or "Woe to":

* *O, my **aching back**\!*  
* *Alas, **my poor heart**\!*  
* *Woe is **me**\!*

In Arabic, **النُّدْبَةُ** creates a poetic and powerful sound that carries the feeling of the pain through the breath.

**النُّدْبَةُ** is a specific type of calling (**مُنَادَى**) used for two reasons:

1. **المُتَفَجَّعُ عَلَيْهِ:** Lamenting someone who has passed away or a great loss.  
2. **المُتَوَجَّعُ مِنْهُ:** Expressing physical pain in a specific body part.

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

* **The Tool:** You must use **وَا** (*Wā*). You cannot use *Yā* for true lamentation.  
* **The Ending:** We add an **Alif** (ـَا) to the end of the noun to show the "stretch" of the cry.  
* **The Final Touch:** We often add a **Hā** (ـه) at the very end when stopping, which is why you hear "Qalb-āh" or "Rā's-āh."  
* **Grammar State:** Just like the **Vocative (Lesson 10.8)**, the noun is fundamentally **مَنْصُوبٌ** (Accusative/Fat-ha). The Alif is added onto that Fat-ha.

#### **Lesson 14.7:** Appeal for Help: (الاِسْتِغَاثَةُ) {#lesson-14.7:-appeal-for-help:-(الاِسْتِغَاثَةُ)}

"Imagine you are lost at sea. You don’t just wave your hand and say 'hello' to a passing ship. You fire an **emergency flare** that has a very specific color and sound so everyone knows it’s a cry for help. In Arabic, **Istighāthah** is that flare. It uses a specific 'Lām' sound that acts as the siren, telling the listener: 'This isn't a normal conversation; someone needs a hero right now.'" 

In Arabic, there is a specific grammatical "S.O.S." signal. It’s not just a regular call; it’s an **Appeal for Help**. It connects the person who can help to the person (or cause) that needs it, all in one short, powerful sentence.

Notice the "Lām" (لـَ) with a Fat-ha on the helper, and the "Lām" (لِـ) with a Kasra on the one being helped:

* **يَا لَلْقَوِيِّ لِلضَّعِيفِ\!** (O \[I call\] the strong **to help** the weak\!)  
* **يَا لَلْأَطِبَّاءِ لِلْمَرْضَى\!** (O \[I call\] the doctors **to help** the sick\!)  
* **يَا لَلْمُسْلِمِينَ لِلْقُدْسِ\!** (O \[I call\] the Muslims **to help** Jerusalem\!)  
* **يَا لَلهِ لِلْمَظْلُومِ\!** (O Allah, \[help\] the oppressed\!)

The **الاِسْتِغَاثَةُ** packs all that urgency into a specific arrangement of the letter **Lām**.

**الاِسْتِغَاثَةُ** is a specialized form of the Vocative (calling). It has three main components:

1. **أَدَاةُ النِّدَاءِ (The Tool):** Always use **يَا**.  
2. **المُسْتَغَاثُ بِهِ (The Helper):** The person you are calling for help. This word gets a **Lām with a Fat-ha** (**لَـ**) attached to the front.  
3. **المُسْتَغَاثُ لَهُ (The Reason/Victim):** The person or cause that needs the help. This word gets a **Lām with a Kasra** (**لِـ**) attached to the front.

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

* **The Helper's Lām:** The Lām attached to the person you are calling **must** have a Fat-ha (**لَـ**).

  *Note:* If you repeat the helper, the second one takes a Kasra.

* **The Victim's Lām:** The Lām attached to the person needing help **must** have a Kasra (**لِـ**).  
* **The "Alif" Alternative:** Sometimes, instead of the first Lām, Arabs add an **Alif** at the end of the helper's name.

  *Example:* **يَا زَيْدَاهُ لِعَمْرٍو\!** (O Zayd, help Amr\!)

* **Grammar State:** Because of the Lām (which is a preposition here), the nouns will be in the **مَجْرُور** (Genitive/Kasra) state.

#### **Lesson 14.8:** Shortening/Curtailing Names: (التَّرْخِيمُ) {#lesson-14.8:-shortening/curtailing-names:-(التَّرْخِيمُ)}

"Imagine you are meeting your boss. You give a full, formal handshake with eye contact and a 'Good morning, Sir.' That's the full name: **يَا مَنْصُورُ** (*Ya Mansuru*).

But when you see your best friend, you might just do a quick **'high-five'** or a half-wave. You don't need the full formality because the connection is already so strong. **Tarkhīm** is that high-five. By dropping the end of the name, you are signaling: *'We are so close that I don't even need to finish your name for you to know I love you.'*"

Have you ever been in such a hurry to call a friend that you didn't even finish their name? Or perhaps you used a "nickname" to show closeness? In English, we might call "Benjamin" just "Ben."*Thomas* → *Tom, Elizabeth* → *Beth* or *Liz,b Alexander* → *Alex*

In Arabic, this isn't just slang; it's a formal grammatical art called **التَّرْخِيمُ** (Tar-kheem), which literally means "softening" or "easing." How do we legally "cut" a name in Arabic while keeping it grammatically correct?

Notice how the names in the underline are "shortened" versions of the original names:

* **يَا عَائِشَ، أَقْبِلِي.** (O **'A'ish**\! — originally **عَائِشَةُ**)  
* **يَا فَاطِمَ، تَمَهَّلِي.** (O **Fatim**\! — originally **فَاطِمَةُ**)  
* **يَا حَارِ، انْتَبِهْ.** (O **Hari**\! — originally **حَارِثُ**)  
* **يَا صَاحِ، جَازَاكَ اللهُ خَيْرًا.** (O **Sāh**\! — originally **صَاحِبِي**)

**التَّرْخِيمُ** is the deletion of the final letter (or letters) of a noun when it is being called (**مُنَادَى**).

There are two ways to pronounce the shortened name:

1. **Language of those who "Wait" (لُغَةُ مَنْ يَنْتَظِرُ):** You keep the original vowel of the letter that is now at the end.  
   * *Example:* For **عَائِشَةُ**, you cut the **ة** and say **يَا عَائِشَ** (with the original Fat-ha).  
2. **Language of those who "Don't Wait" (لُغَةُ مَنْ لَا يَنْتَظِرُ):** You treat the new end of the word as a fresh start and give it a **Damma**.  
   * *Example:* You cut the **ة** and say **يَا عَائِشُ**.

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

1. **The Calling Tool:** It only happens after **يَا**.  
2. **What can be shortened?**  
   * Any name ending in **ة** (like **خَدِيجَة** or **حَمْزَة**), regardless of length.  
   * Names that are more than 3 letters long and are proper nouns (like **سُعَاد** → **يَا سُعَا**).  
3. **The "One Letter" Rule:** Usually, only the last letter is deleted. However, if the second-to-last letter is a "weak" letter (Alif, Waw, or Ya), both might be deleted (e.g., **مَنْصُور** → **يَا مَنْصُ**).  
4. **Purpose:** It is used for making the speech sweet/friendly or because the name is long and needs to be shortened for speed.

#### **Lesson 15 .1 :** Oaths: (القَسَمُ) {#lesson-15-.1-:-oaths:-(القَسَمُ)}

Imagine you are sending a top-secret letter in ancient times. If you just fold the paper, anyone can open it and claim the message isn't real. To prove it’s from the King, you pour hot wax on the seam and press a **Royal Signet Ring** into it. 

Or 

Have you ever wanted to convince someone that you are telling the absolute truth? You might say, "I swear\!" or "Honestly\!"

In Arabic, taking an **Oath** is a very serious and structured act. It isn't just about the words you say; the grammar itself changes to show the weight of your promise. How does "Allah" become "Allahi" just by adding a single letter of swearing? 

Notice the special "Swearing Letters" at the start and the **Kasra** (ـِ) on the name of Allah:

* **وَاللهِ، لَأَقُولَنَّ الحَقَّ.** (**By Allah**, I will certainly speak the truth.)  
* **تَاللهِ لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللهُ عَلَيْنَا.** (**By Allah**, indeed Allah has preferred you over us.)  
* **بِاللهِ، هَلْ رَأَيْتَ زَيْدًا؟** (**By Allah**, did you see Zayd?)

In English, we use "By," "I swear by," or "I take an oath":

* ***By** God\!*  
* ***I swear by** my honor.*  
* ***By** the sun and the moon\!*

An **Oath (القَسَمُ**) consists of three parts:

1. **أَدَاةُ القَسَمِ (The Swearing Tool):** The letters used to start the oath.  
2. **المُقْسَمُ بِهِ (The One Sworn By):** In Islam, this is only Allah or His attributes.  
3. **جَوَابُ القَسَمِ (The Statement):** The thing you are actually swearing is true.

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

* **The Kasra Rule:** The tools of oath (**و، ب، ت**) are actually a type of **Preposition (حُرُوفُ الجَرِّ**). This means the word after them **must** take a **Kasra**.

  *Correct:* **وَاللهِ** (Wallahi). *Incorrect:* **وَاللهُ** (Wallahu).

* **The "Lām" of Certainty:** The statement you are swearing (the result) often starts with a **لَـ** to add even more emphasis.

  *Example:* **وَاللهِ لَـأَجْتَهِدَنَّ** (By Allah, I will **surely** work hard).

* **The "Nūn" of Emphasis:** Verbs in an oath often end with a heavy **نّ** (Nūn al-Tawkīd).  
* **Omitting the Verb:** Usually, we don't say "I swear" (**أُقْسِمُ**); the letter **و** or **ب** carries that meaning on its own.

#### **Lesson 15 .3 :** Interrogation: (الاِسْتِفْهَامُ) {#lesson-15-.3-:-interrogation:-(الاِسْتِفْهَامُ)}

What is the most important tool for a scientist, a detective, or a student? It is the **Question**. Without questions, we cannot learn\!

In Arabic, how do we turn a regular statement into a question? Do we just change our tone of voice, or are there specific "Keys of Knowledge" we use to unlock answers? Let's explore the world of **Interrogation**\!

Notice the "Question Tools" at the very beginning of each sentence:

* **هَلْ أَنْتَ بَيِّنٌ؟** (**Are** you clear?)  
* **مَنْ هَذَا الرَّجُلُ؟** (**Who** is this man?)  
* **مَاذَا تَفْعَلُ؟** (**What** are you doing?)  
* **كَيْفَ حَالُكَ؟** (**How** are you?)  
* **أَقَرَأْتَ الكِتَابَ؟** (**Did** you read the book?)

In English, we use "Wh- words" or change the order of the sentence:

* *You are a student.* → ***Are** you a student?*  
* ***Where** is the library?*  
* ***When** is the exam?*

**الاِسْتِفْهَامُ** is seeking knowledge about something that was previously unknown. Arabic question tools are divided into two types:

1. **Letters (حَرْفَان):** These are **هَلْ** and **أَ**. They are used for "Yes/No" questions.  
2. **Nouns (أَسْمَاء):** These are used to ask about specific things (people, places, times, reasons).

| Tool (الأَدَاة) | Meaning/Use (المَعْنَى) |
| :---- | :---- |
| **مَنْ** | Who (For people) |
| **مَا / مَاذَا** | What (For objects/actions) |
| **مَتَى** | When (For time) |
| **أَيْنَ** | Where (For place) |
| **كَيْفَ** | How (For state/manner) |
| **كَمْ** | How many (For quantity) |
| **لِمَاذَا** | Why (For reason) |

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

* **Priority of Position:** The question tool **must** be the first word in the sentence.  
* **The "Hal" vs "Hamza" Rule:**

  **هَلْ** is for simple Yes/No questions.

  **أَ** can be used for Yes/No, but it is also used to ask for a **choice** between two things (using **أَمْ** \- "or").

  *Example:* **أَ**طَالِبٌ أَنْتَ **أَمْ** مُعَلِّمٌ؟ (Are you a student or a teacher?)

* **The "Kam" Rule: are of 2 types**  The noun after **كَمْ** (How many) must be singular, indefinite, and take a **Fat-ha**. But can take kasra after min eg

  *Example:* **كَمْ كِتَابًا عِنْدَكَ؟** (How many books do you have?)

1\. The Question (Interrogative)  
Used to ask **"How many?"**

* **The Rule:** Singular \+ Fatha ($ً$).  
* **Example:** كَمْ **قَلَمًا** مَعَكَ؟  
  *(How many pens do you have?)*

---

2\. The Exclamation (Informative)  
Used to say **"So many\!"**

* **The Rule:** Singular/Plural \+ **مِنْ** \+ Kasra ($ٍ$).  
* **Example:** كَمْ **مِنْ قَلَمٍ** عِنْدِي\!  
  *(How many pens I have\! — meaning: I have tons of pens.)*

#### **15.4 Praise and Blame: (المَدْحُ وَالذَّمُّ)** {#15.4-praise-and-blame:-(المَدْحُ-وَالذَّمُّ)}

How do you give a "Thumbs Up" or a "Thumbs Down" using only your words? If you see a beautiful character trait like honesty, or a bad one like lying, how do you express your strong opinion in a formal, powerful way?

In Arabic, we have a specific grammatical "Scale" for **Praise** (giving credit) and **Blame** (pointing out a fault). It’s the ultimate way to declare what is "Excellent" and what is "Evil."

* **نِعْمَ الخُلُقُ الصِّدْقُ.** (**Excellent** is the character trait: Honesty.)  
* **حَبَّذَا النَّظَافَةُ.** (**Wonderful** is cleanliness.)  
* **بِئْسَ الرَّجُلُ الكَاذِبُ.** (**Evil/Wretched** is the man who lies.)  
* **لَا حَبَّذَا الإِهْمَالُ.** (**How bad** is negligence.)

In English, we usually use adjectives or exclamations:

* ***What a great** friend you are\!*  
* ***Bravo** to your hard work\!*  
* ***Shame** on dishonesty\!*

In Arabic, these specific verbs (**نِعْمَ** and **بِئْسَ**) act as a complete "judgment machine" for the noun that follows.

The Pillar of Praise (المَدْح):

* **نِعْمَ (Ni'ma):** The classic verb for "What a great..."  
* **حَبَّذَا (Habbadhā):** A quicker way to say "This is lovable/excellent."

The Pillar of Blame (الذَّمّ):

* **بِئْسَ (Bi'sa):** The classic verb for "What a wretched..."  
* **لَا حَبَّذَا (Lā Habbadhā):** The opposite of Habbadhā; "This is unlovable/awful."

5\. القَوَاعِدُ (Rules)

1. **The Three Parts:** These sentences have a specific structure: نعم الصديق زيد  
   * **The Verb:** (**نِعْمَ** or **بِئْسَ**).  
   * **The Subject (الفَاعِل):** The category you are judging (e.g., "The friend").  
   * **The Specific Choice (المَخْصُوص):** The exact person or thing you are praising/blaming (e.g., "Zayd").  
2. **The Damma Rule:** All three parts of the sentence typically stay in the **مَرْفُوع** (Damma) state.  
3. **Gender Matching:** If you are praising a female, you can add a silent **ت** to the verb.  
   * *Example:* **نِعْمَتِ المَرْأَةُ مَرْيَمُ.** (Excellent is the woman, Maryam.)  
4. **Implicit Context:** Sometimes we leave out the "Specific Choice" if everyone knows who we are talking about.  
   * *Example:* **نِعْمَ العَبْدُ\!** (What an excellent servant\!) — referring to Prophet Ayyub in the Quran.

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern IV (*If'aal*) is created by adding a fixed Hamza at the beginning of a 3-letter root.

1. **Causative Meaning:** This is the most common use.  
   * *Basic:* **خَرَجَ** (He went out).  
   * *Expanded:* **أَخْرَجَ** (He *made* something go out / He took it out).  
2. **The Present Tense Dammah:** Unlike the basic 3-letter verb (which starts with a Fatha like *Yashrabu*), Expanded Pattern IV verbs **always** start the present tense with a **Dammah** (**يُـ / تُـ / أُـ / نُـ**).  
   * *Correct:* **يُسْلِمُ** (He submits).  
   * *Incorrect:* *Yaslimu*.  
3. **The Masdar:** The noun form follows the rhythm of **إِفْعَال**. This is where words like *Islam*, *Ikhlas*, and *Infaq* come from.

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern II (*Taf’eel*) is formed by doubling the second root letter.

1. **Causative Action:** Like *If'aal*, this pattern makes a verb transitive.  
   * **عَلِمَ** (He knew) → **عَلَّمَ** (He *made* someone know / He taught).  
2. **Intensity/Frequency:** It can imply that an action is done repeatedly or with great force.  
   * **قَطَعَ** (He cut) → **قَطَّعَ** (He chopped/shredded into many pieces).  
3. **The Present Tense Dammah:** Just like Pattern IV (*If'aal*), all "Increased by One Letter" verbs start their present tense with a **Dammah** (**يُـ**).  
4. **The Masdar Pattern:** The noun form is very famous and predictable: **تَفْعِيل**. This is where we get words like *Tafseer*, *Tajweed*, and *Tableeg*.

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern III (*Mufa’alah*) is formed by adding an Alif of "partnership" after the first root letter.

1. **Reciprocity (المُشَارَكَة):** Most verbs in this pattern involve at least two people.  
   * **ضَرَبَ** (He hit).  
   * **ضَارَبَ** (He traded/exchanged blows with someone).  
2. **The Present Tense Dammah:** This is the last of the "Group of 4" (Patterns I, II, III, IV). Remember: if the past tense has 4 letters (counting the Alif or Shaddah), the present tense **must** start with a **Dammah** (**يُـ**).  
3. **The Masdar Patterns:**  
   * The primary pattern is **مُفَاعَلَة** (e.g., *Musa'adah* \- Help).  
   * Some verbs also have a second pattern: **فِعَال** (e.g., *Jihad*, *Qital*, *Hisab*).

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern V (*Tafau'ul*) is formed by adding a **تـ** to Pattern II.

1. **Reflexive/Resultant (المُطَاوَعَة):** This pattern shows the result of an action.  
   * *Pattern II:* **كَسَّرْتُ الزُّجَاجَ** (I smashed the glass).  
   * *Pattern V:* **تَكَسَّرَ الزُّجَاجُ** (The glass smashed/became smashed).  
2. **The Present Tense Fatha:** Notice a major change\! In the "Group of One" (Patterns II, III, IV), the present tense started with a **Dammah** (*Yu-*). But for the "Group of Two" (Patterns V and VI), the present tense starts with a **Fatha** (**يَتَـ**).  
3. **The Masdar Dammah:** The noun form looks exactly like the past tense, except the vowel on the middle letter changes from a Fatha to a **Dammah** (**تَفَعُّـل**).  
   * Past: **تَوَكَّلَ** (He relied).  
   * Noun: **تَوَكُّل** (Reliance).

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern VI (*Tafaa’ul*) is formed by adding a **تـ** to Pattern III.

1. **Mutual Action (المُشَارَكَة):** While Pattern III can be one-sided (e.g., "He fought him"), Pattern VI is always mutual (e.g., "They fought each other"). It often requires a plural subject or the word "between" (*bayna*).  
2. **The Present Tense Fatha:** Like Pattern V, the present tense prefix is **يَتَـ** (Fatha).  
3. **The Masdar Dammah:** Just like the previous pattern, the noun is identical to the past tense, except for a **Dammah** on the penultimate letter.  
   * Past: **تَعَاوَنَ** (He cooperated).  
   * Noun: **تَعَاوُن** (Cooperation).  
4. **Pretending:** Interestingly, this pattern is also used to express "pretending" to be in a state.  
   * **مَرِضَ** (He got sick).  
   * **تَمَارَضَ** (He pretended to be sick).

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern VII (*Infi’aal*) is formed by adding **انـ** to the start of the 3-letter root.

1. **Passive Result (المُطَاوَعَة):** This pattern is the "direct result" of an action done by something else.  
   * *Action (Pattern I):* **كَسَرْتُ القَلَمَ** (I broke the pen).  
   * *Result (Pattern VII):* **انْكَسَرَ القَلَمُ** (The pen broke / became broken).  
2. **No Direct Object:** Because the action is self-contained within the subject, you will almost never find a **Maf'ul Bihi** (Object) in an *Infi'aal* sentence.  
3. **Hamzatul Wasl:** The Alif at the beginning is a "connecting Alif." If you say a word before it, the "i" sound disappears (e.g., *wa-nkisara* instead of *wa inkisara*).  
4. **Present Tense:** Like the patterns we just saw (V and VI), the present tense starts with a **Fatha** (**يَنْـ**).

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern VIII (*Ifti’aal*) is formed by adding an **Alif** at the start and a **Taa** between the 1st and 2nd root letters.

1. **Reflexive/Effort (الاِجْتِهَاد):** Often implies doing the action for oneself or with a degree of effort.  
   * *Basic:* **جَمَعَ** (He gathered something).  
   * *Ifti'aal:* **اجْتَمَعَ** (They gathered together/met).  
2. **The "T" Shift (Advanced):** If the first root letter is a "heavy" letter like **ص** or **ط**, the **ت** in the pattern often changes to a **ط** to make it easier to say (e.g., *Sabbara* → **اصْطَبَرَ**).  
3. **Present Tense:** Like the other "Group of Two" patterns, the present tense prefix takes a **Fatha** (**يَفْتَـ**).  
4. **The Masdar:** Always follows the rhythmic **افْتِعَال** (e.g., *Ijtimaa'*, *Ikhtilaaf*, *Ibtikaar*).

IV. Grammar Rules & Explanation (القَوَاعِدُ وَالشَّرْحُ)  
**The Rule:** Pattern IX (*If'ilaal*) is formed by adding a starting Alif and doubling the final root letter.

1. **Colors and Defects (الأَلْوَان وَالعُيُوب):** This pattern is specialized. It doesn't mean "to do" something, but "to become" a certain color or state.  
2. **The Masdar Change:** In the past and present, the last two letters are merged (Shaddah). However, in the **Masdar** (Noun form), an Alif is inserted between them, forcing them to separate.  
   * Past: **احْمَرَّ**  
   * Masdar: **احْمِـرَار** (The two 'R's are separated by the Alif).  
3. **Intransitive:** Like Pattern VII, these verbs are self-contained. You don't "red" something; you *become* red. Therefore, no Object (**Maf'ul Bihi**) is needed.

8.2.09: اسْتِفْعَال (The "Seeking" Pattern)

This is the most functional pattern. It adds **ا-س-تـ** to the start.

* **Meaning:** Usually "To seek" or "To request" the root.  
* **Example:** **غَفَرَ** (To forgive) → **اسْتَغْفَرَ** (To seek forgiveness).  
* **Present:** **يَسْتَفْعِلُ** (Starts with Fatha).

8.2.10 \- 8.2.12: The Intensive Patterns

These are rare and mostly found in classical poetry or specific descriptions.

* **If'ee'aal (8.2.10):** Adds Alif, Waw, and repeats the 2nd root letter (e.g., **اعْشَوْشَبَ** \- extremely grassy).  
* **If'eelaal (8.2.11):** An even stronger version of Pattern IX for colors (e.g., **احْمَارَّ** \- turned deep, vibrant red).  
* **If'iwwaal (8.2.12):** Adds Alif and a doubled Waw (e.g., **اجْلِوَّذَ** \- to bolt or speed intensely).

---

IV. Grammar Rules (القَوَاعِدُ)

1. **The Hamza:** All these patterns start with **Hamzatul Wasl** (the connecting Alif). The "i" sound is dropped if connected to a previous word.  
2. **Present Tense Prefix:** Unlike the "Increased by One" group (which used Dammah), these long patterns all start with **Fatha** in the present tense (**يَسْتَـ**, **يَعْشَـ**, etc.).  
3. **The Masdar:** The Masdar for Pattern X (*Istif'aal*) is very common: **اسْتِفْعَال**.  
   * *Example:* **اسْتَقْبَلَ** (He received) → **اسْتِقْبَال** (Reception).

III. Concept Analysis: The Four Irregulars  
**Purpose:** To visualize where the "weakness" or "irregularity" occurs in the root.

1\. Al-Mahmuz (المَهْمُوز)

The Hamza is a strong consonant, but its spelling changes based on the vowels around it.

* **Start:** **أَكَلَ** (He ate)  
* **Middle:** **سَأَلَ** (He asked)  
* **End:** **قَرَأَ** (He read)

2\. Al-Muda'af (المُضَاعَف)

When the 2nd and 3rd letters are the same, they merge (Idgham) with a Shaddah.

* **Past:** **شَدَّ** (He pulled) — originally *Shadada*.  
* **Present:** **يَشُدُّ** (He pulls).

3\. Al-Mu'tal (المُعْتَلّ) \- The Weak Verbs

This is where the real "Scholarship" begins. Depending on where the weak letter (**و** or **ي**) is, the verb has a different name:

* **Mithal (Start):** **وَجَدَ** (He found). The "Waw" often drops in the present: **يَجِدُ**.  
* **Ajwaf (Middle):** **قَالَ** (He said). The middle letter turns into an Alif.  
* **Naqis (End):** **دَعَا** (He called). The end letter changes based on the suffix
